### Brundtland Definition (1987) - MEMORIZE

- Needs-based framing (not wants)

## 3. The IPAT Equation

### Formula: I = P × A × T

| Variable | Meaning | Description |
|----------|---------|-------------|
| **I** | Impact | Total environmental burden |
| **P** | Population | Number of people |
| **A** | Affluence | Material standard of living (GDP/person) |
| **T** | Technology Factor | Environmental intensity (Impact/GDP) |

**Key scholars:** Ehrlich & Holdren (1971), Commoner (1972), Graedel & Allenby (1995)

### Climate Change Calculation Example
- Need to reduce Impact (I) to 0.5 by 2030
- Population (P) increasing by factor 1.1
- Affluence (A) increasing by factor 1.3
- **T = 0.5 / (1.1 × 1.3) = 0.35** → Technology must improve by **Factor 3**

### Eco-Efficiency
**Eco-efficiency = 1/T** (Delivered Service / Environmental Impact)

"Creating more with less"

## 7. SDG Methodology: 5 Phases

### Overview

| Phase | Name | Key Question |
|-------|------|--------------|
| **1** | Scoping | What are the system boundaries? |
| **2** | Process Diagrams | What processes are in each system? |
| **3** | Effects | What changes between baseline and new system? |
| **4** | Assessment | How do effects impact SDGs? |
| **5** | Interpretation | What recommendations follow? |

**Critical feature:** The methodology is **iterative** - return to earlier phases as understanding develops.

---

### Phase 1: Scoping

**Three Scoping Parameters:**

| Parameter | Question | Options |
|-----------|----------|---------|
| **Time** | When to compare? | Historical vs. future scenario |
| **Application** | How much to cover? | Sub-system level (not too broad/narrow) |
| **Geography** | Where? | Country, region, or local area |

**Key concepts:**
- **Baseline system:** Current/existing situation
- **New system:** Proposed alternative
- **Functional output:** Equivalent function both systems must deliver

---

### Phase 2: Process Diagrams

Create visual flowcharts showing all processes in baseline and new systems, organized by life cycle stage.

**Marking changes from baseline to new:**
- **Added process** (new in new system)
- **Removed process** (was in baseline)
- **Increased process** (same, larger scale)
- **Reduced process** (same, smaller scale)

---

### Phase 3: Identifying Effects

**Effects** = Differences between baseline and new system

#### Classification Dimensions

| Dimension 1 | Definition |
|-------------|------------|
| **Direct effects** | Intended, reflect purpose of change |
| **Indirect effects** | Unintended consequences |

| Dimension 2 | Definition |
|-------------|------------|
| **Physical effects** | Measurable changes (emissions, materials) |
| **Non-physical effects** | Social/behavioral changes (working conditions, education) |

---

### Phase 4: Assessment

#### Step 1: Causality Chains
Link effects to SDGs: **Effect → Consequences → SDG Impacts**

#### Step 2: Semi-Quantitative Scoring

**Likelihood Scale:**
| Category | Probability |
|----------|-------------|
| Likely | **> 67%** |
| Possible | **33-67%** |
| Unlikely | **< 33%** |

**Size/Magnitude Scale:**
| Category | Range |
|----------|-------|
| Major | **50-100%** |
| Moderate | **20-50%** |
| Minor | **5-20%** |
| Negligible | **0-5%** |

**Scoring Matrix:**

| Size \ Likelihood | Likely | Possible | Unlikely |
|-------------------|--------|----------|----------|
| **Major** | +++/--- | ++/-- | +/- |
| **Moderate** | ++/-- | +/- | 0 |
| **Minor** | +/- | 0 | 0 |
| **Negligible** | 0 | 0 | 0 |

---

### Phase 5: Interpretation & Recommendations

**Three Deliverables:**

1. **Most Important Impacts:** 3 positive + 3 negative SDG impacts
2. **Trade-offs:** SDG interrelations and improvement possibilities
3. **Recommendations:** Specific, actionable, for relevant stakeholders

**Stakeholder categories:** Politicians, Companies, Users, Researchers, NGOs

**Trade-off definition:** When improving one SDG worsens another.

## 1. Responsible Research and Innovation (RRI)

### Why RRI? The Nature of Wicked Problems

**Key scholars:** Rittel & Webber (1973)

**Wicked Problems** are societal challenges that resist simple solutions because:
- No undisputable public good exists
- No objective definition of equity
- No "optimal solutions" - only better or worse

### Five Characteristics of Wicked Problems - MEMORIZE

| Characteristic | Description | Example |
|----------------|-------------|---------|
| **1. No stopping rule** | Problems are ongoing; solutions become new interventions | Poverty alleviation - causes constantly change |
| **2. Solutions are irreversible** | Path dependence; little room to experiment | EV infrastructure investment |
| **3. Problem description determines solutions** | Framing controversies; different stakeholders frame differently | Is circumcision religious, medical, or legal? |
| **4. Nested problems** | Problems are symptoms of other wicked problems | Unemployment ↔ AI development |
| **5. Better/worse, not true/false** | Solutions benefit some groups while disadvantaging others | Urban gentrification |

---

## 2. The AREA Framework for RRI - MEMORIZE

**Developed by:** UK Engineering and Physical Sciences Research Council (EPSRC)
**Based on:** Stilgoe, Owen, Macnaghten (2013)

| Dimension | Question | Focus |
|-----------|----------|-------|
| **A**nticipate | What if...? | Explore possible impacts (intended AND unintended) |
| **R**eflect | Whose assumptions? | Consider purpose, motivations, framings |
| **E**ngage | With whom? | Open discussions to relevant societal stakeholders |
| **A**ct | How to respond? | Use these processes to influence research direction |

### Anticipate
- Take **uncertainty seriously** - hard to predict effects of emerging technologies
- Work **against the impulse** to narrow imagination to your expertise
- Ask: Is this socially desirable? For which stakeholders? In what scenarios?

### Reflect
- Reflect on **assumptions**, **problem framing**, and **motivations**
- Key questions:
  - Who has a **stake** in the research?
  - Who has **influence**?
  - Who is **responsible** for harmful consequences?

### Engage
- Invite stakeholders to participate in anticipation and reflection
- Ask if they share your assumptions, framing, and views on accountability

### Act
- Requires: interdisciplinary training, technology assessment methods, structural incentives

## 3. Normative Ethics

### Three Main Traditions - MEMORIZE

| Tradition | Focus | Key Question | Blind Spots |
|-----------|-------|--------------|-------------|
| **Consequentialism** | Outcomes | Does it maximize utility/wellbeing? | Rights, justice, autonomy |
| **Deontology** | Duties/Rights | Are there inviolable principles? | Rigidity when duties collide |
| **Virtue Ethics** | Character | Does it enable a good life? | Less clear for large-scale trade-offs |

### Consequentialism (Outcomes)
- **Act consequentialism:** Judge each action by its consequences
- **Rule consequentialism:** Judge rules by outcomes they tend to produce
- **Effective Altruism:** Maximize good with limited resources using evidence-based metrics
- **Strength:** Measurement-heavy methods (LCA uses consequentialist logic)
- **Limitation:** May neglect rights, justice, structural inequality

### Deontology (Duties)
**Key scholar:** Immanuel Kant

**Kant's Categorical Imperative:**
1. **Universality:** Act only on maxims you could will as universal law
2. **Humanity:** Never treat persons merely as means

**Rawls' Veil of Ignorance:** Design principles without knowing your position
- **Greatest Equal Liberty Principle:** Protect freedoms that don't infringe others
- **Difference Principle:** Inequalities only justified if they benefit least advantaged

### Virtue Ethics (Character)
- Focus on cultivating virtuous character (e.g., courage between cowardice and recklessness)
- **Care Ethics:** Builds responsibility from relations of dependence and responsiveness to needs
- Relevant for professional identity and relational responsibility

## 4. Politics of Technology

### Three Myths Challenged

| Myth | Challenge | Concept |
|------|-----------|---------|
| Technology is autonomous | Technology is shaped by human action | **Interpretative flexibility** |
| Technology is neutral | Design prescribes certain uses | **Script** |
| Technology is inherently good | Technology has political characteristics | **Political technologies** |

### Interpretative Flexibility (Kline & Pinch)
- Different social groups hold different interpretations of what a technology is/does
- **Closure:** Over time, one interpretation stabilizes as dominant through social processes
- **Key insight:** Counters "technology is inevitable" narratives

### Script (Akrich)
> "Designers inscribe assumptions about users' competences, motives, and 'proper' ways of acting into technical objects"

**User responses (de-scription):**
- Follow
- Negotiate
- Repurpose
- Resist

**Key insight:** Scripts make "technology is neutral" hard to sustain

### Political Technologies (Winner)

| Type | Definition | Example |
|------|------------|---------|
| **Explicitly political** | Deliberately designed to produce/reinforce power relations | Tomato harvester favoring large agribusiness |
| **Inherently political** | Technical requirements make certain governance forms necessary | Nuclear power requires centralization, hierarchy, security |

## 5. Expertise

### Paradigms and Normal Science (Kuhn)

**Paradigm:** A shared framework organizing what problems matter, what methods are legitimate, what counts as evidence

**Thought Collective:** Group stabilized by shared assumptions and practices

**Normal Science:** "Puzzle-solving" within the paradigm

**Paradigm Shift:** When anomalies accumulate and alternative emerges; often led by junior scholars

**Key insight:** Disagreements persist not from "information deficits" but from competing paradigms

### Expertise Types (Collins & Evans) - MEMORIZE

| Type | Definition | Role |
|------|------------|------|
| **Contributory expertise** | Full mastery of language AND practice | Produce specialized knowledge |
| **Interactional expertise** | Fluency in discourse WITHOUT practical ability | Enable cross-domain cooperation |

### Trading Zones
- Spaces where groups with different languages/cultures collaborate
- Develop partial shared vocabularies ("inter-languages")
- Can be cooperative or coercive

## 6. Risk and Uncertainty

### Risk Assessment
**Risk = Likelihood × Severity** of adverse consequences

**Two types of unknowns:**
- **Known unknowns:** Risks we're aware of but can't quantify (Adams, 1995)
- **Unknown unknowns:** Risks we don't even know exist (Rumsfeld, 2002)

### Cultural Theory of Risk (Mary Douglas) - MEMORIZE

**Core argument:** Risk perception is shaped by cultural and social factors, not purely objective calculation

### Grid/Group Model - MEMORIZE

**Two dimensions:**
- **Grid:** How strongly constrained by rules and hierarchy
- **Group:** How strongly bound by collective identity

### Four Risk Perception Types

| Type | Grid | Group | View of Nature | Risk Approach | Trust |
|------|------|-------|----------------|---------------|-------|
| **Hierarchist** | High | High | Tolerant within limits | "Manage and regulate" | Authorities, experts |
| **Individualist** | Low | Low | Resilient | "Go for it" | Markets, self |
| **Egalitarian** | Low | High | Vulnerable | "Better safe than sorry" | Community, grassroots |
| **Fatalist** | High | Low | Capricious | "Come what may" | Nothing/fate |

**Key insight:** Different risk perceptions explain why debates persist despite scientific evidence

## 7. Controversy Mapping Methodology

### What is Controversy Mapping?
- Method for the **Engagement** component of AREA framework
- Opens anticipation and reflection to wider stakeholders
- Answers: Who are the actors? What are their concerns? How is it changing?

### 17 Key Methodological Notions - Summary Table

| Notion | Scholar | Core Idea |
|--------|---------|-----------|
| **Post-demographics** | Rogers | Study digital traces, not "real" people |
| **Just observe** | Latour | Observe without imposing theory |
| **Second-degree objectivity** | Latour | Be objective about what actors SAY, not which facts are true |
| **Exploratory Data Analysis** | - | Find questions, not answers |
| **Close/distant reading** | Moretti | Close = traditional; Distant = transform data to reveal patterns |
| **Quali-quantitative** | Venturini | Alternate between qualitative and quantitative moments |
| **Principle of symmetry** | Bloor | Apply same methods to all knowledge claims |
| **Actor (ANT)** | Latour | Someone/something that makes a difference |
| **Follow the actors** | Latour | Listen to actors' terms and logic, not your own |
| **Weighing positions** | - | Show which actors make bigger differences |
| **Measurements** | - | Making social life knowable and comparable |
| **Circulating reference** | Latour | Chain of transformations maintaining connection |
| **Truth and robustness** | Latour | Arguments become true as they become robust |
| **Toulmin model** | Toulmin | Six-part argument structure |
| **Technical mediation** | Latour | Technology translates, not just transmits |
| **Explanatory visualization** | - | Communicate context to audience |
| **Annotation** | - | Guide reader's eyes to visual patterns |

### Key Methodological Principles

**Second-Degree Objectivity:**
- First-degree objectivity (agreement on facts) is impossible in controversies
- Be objective about **what actors claim**, not which claims are true

**Principle of Symmetry (Bloor):**
- Same explanations for successful AND unsuccessful knowledge claims
- Analyze "flat earthers" with same tools as physicists
- **BUT:** Fair treatment ≠ equal weight (weighing positions shows differential impact)

**Follow the Actors (Latour):**
- Use actors' terminology, not yours
- Follow them to uncomfortable places you disagree with
- Pay attention to their specific terms and arguments

### Toulmin Argument Model - MEMORIZE

| Component | Definition | Example |
|-----------|------------|---------|
| **Claim** | Statement that something is so | "The Earth is round" |
| **Grounds** | Data backing the claim | "Earth's shadow on Moon is round" |
| **Warrant** | WHY claim follows from grounds | "Shadows indicate shape" |
| **Backing** | Support for the warrant | "Spheres cast round shadows" |
| **Qualifier** | Degree of certainty | "We can reasonably consider..." |
| **Rebuttal** | Exceptions | "Unless Earth is a flat disc facing sun" |

**Key takeaway:** Robust arguments require explicit **warrant** and **backing**, not just claim + data

### Two Data Approaches

| Approach | Origin | Method | Exclusivity |
|----------|--------|--------|-------------|
| **Top-down (Issues)** | Researchers define | AI codes against issue dictionary | Non-exclusive (0, 1, or multiple) |
| **Bottom-up (Clusters)** | Actors define | Semantic similarity grouping | Mutually exclusive |

## 8. Social Acceptance

### Definition
Willingness of individuals, communities, and stakeholders to support and integrate a technology into society.

### Factors Influencing Acceptance (Huijts et al., 2012)

| Factor | Description |
|--------|-------------|
| **Trust** | Confidence in regulators and developers |
| **Fairness** | Perceived equitable distribution of benefits/burdens |
| **Affect** | Emotional reactions |
| **Perceived costs/benefits/risks** | Assessment of impacts |
| **Efficacy** | Belief in technology's effectiveness |
| **Social norms** | Alignment with societal values |
| **Personal norms** | Alignment with personal beliefs |

### Two Levels of Acceptance
- **Site/project-specific:** Local community concerns about specific project
- **General population:** Broader societal perceptions

### Key Findings from CCS Research (Ladenburg)
1. **Information provision can increase acceptance** but effects are context-sensitive
2. **Prior familiarity** consistently increases acceptance and willingness to pay
3. **Location matters** - different info affects acceptance differently by location
4. **Tailored communication strategies** needed for different groups

## 10. Universal Design

### Definition
Designing products and environments **inherently accessible and usable by all people**, regardless of abilities or age - building inclusion into baseline design from the start.

**vs. Accessibility:** Retrofitting solutions for specific groups after the fact

### Seven Principles of Universal Design - MEMORIZE

| Principle | Description | Example |
|-----------|-------------|---------|
| **1. Equitable use** | Useful to people with different abilities | Level train boarding |
| **2. Flexibility in use** | Accommodates wide range of preferences | Adjustable desk heights |
| **3. Simple and intuitive** | Easy to understand regardless of experience | IKEA pictorial instructions |
| **4. Perceptible information** | Communicates effectively regardless of conditions | Multi-sensory restroom signs |
| **5. Tolerance for error** | Minimizes hazards of unintended actions | "Undo" function |
| **6. Low physical effort** | Efficient and comfortable with minimum fatigue | Automatic doors |
| **7. Size and space** | Appropriate for approach/reach/manipulation | Wide metro turnstiles |

### Key Insight
**Designing for the margins creates better products for everyone** (curb cuts, electric toothbrushes, dropped kerbs)

## 11. Users and Non-Users

### Wyatt's Four Categories of Non-Users - MEMORIZE

| Category | Definition | Type |
|----------|------------|------|
| **Resisters** | Never use because they don't want to | Voluntary non-adoption |
| **Rejecters** | Stopped using voluntarily | Voluntary abandonment |
| **Excluded** | Lack access for various reasons | Involuntary non-adoption |
| **Expelled** | Stopped using involuntarily | Involuntary abandonment |

**Key insight:** Different categories require **different policy and design responses**

### The Weaver's Problem
Challenge of bridging distance between designers and users created by mass production

### Persistent Constraints in Technology Development
1. Viewing social groups and technology as separate
2. **Assuming the user is everybody**
3. **I-methodology** (designing based on oneself)
4. Not considering **scripts** and embedded assumptions
5. Not accounting for non-acceptance and resistance

### Key Lesson for Engineers
> **"If you only design for users, you may unintentionally design AGAINST others."**

## 12. Public Engagement with Science and Technology

### Evolution of Approaches

| Era | Approach | Model | Public Role |
|-----|----------|-------|-------------|
| **1980s** | Public Understanding of Science (PUS) | Deficit model | Passive recipients |
| **1990s+** | Public Engagement with Science (PES) | Dialogue model | Active participants |

### Information Deficit Hypothesis (Critique)
- Assumption: Resistance comes from lack of knowledge
- Problem: Ignores legitimate concerns, values, and lay expertise

### Callon's Three Models of Participation

| Model | Knowledge View | Public Role | Goal |
|-------|----------------|-------------|------|
| **1. Education** | Lay and expert knowledge mutually exclusive | Passive | Make public literate |
| **2. Consultation** | Scientific knowledge incomplete; lay knowledge completes it | Consulted | Address affected concerns |
| **3. Co-creation** | Separation advantages some, disadvantages others | Active participants | Involve public in knowledge production |

### Upstream vs. Downstream Engagement - MEMORIZE

| Type | Timing | Impact Potential |
|------|--------|------------------|
| **Upstream** | Early in development | Higher - can shape direction |
| **Downstream** | Late in development | Lower - mainly acceptance/rejection |

**Key insight:** Involving society **early** improves innovation quality and social support

### Public Engagement Methods
- Consensus conferences
- Citizens' juries
- Deliberative polling
- Focus groups
- Citizen science (e.g., Foldit, eBird)
- Value-sensitive design
- Constructive Technology Assessment (CTA)

## 13. Visual Storytelling

### Exploratory vs. Explanatory Visualization

| Type | Purpose | Audience | Stage |
|------|---------|----------|-------|
| **Exploratory** | Discover patterns | Yourself | Analysis phase |
| **Explanatory** | Communicate message | External | Final communication |

### Technical Mediation
Visualization is **never neutral** - design choices constitute mediation:
- Axis scaling
- Color choices
- Data selection
- Chart type

### Five-Step Visual Argumentation
1. **Produce** the visualization
2. **Provide context** (title, labels, legends)
3. **Point** to relevant visual pattern
4. **Interpret** what the pattern means
5. **Justify** with larger context

### Annotation Principle
> Annotations should be **in-place**: close to what they refer to

## Quick Reference: RRI Strategy for Policy Briefs

### The RRI Logic
> RRI is about **anticipating how your innovation can be a problem in society**. To do so, you **reflect on the limits of your own assumptions** by **engaging stakeholders who see it differently**.

### Policy Brief Structure

| Section | Approach | Concept Types Used |
|---------|----------|-------------------|
| **Methodology** | Quantitatively map actors/issues | Methodological concepts |
| **Analysis** | Qualitatively analyze statements | Analytical concepts |
| **Recommendation** | Suggest RRI strategy | Interventional concepts |

### Three Concept Types

| Type | Purpose | Rule |
|------|---------|------|
| **Methodological** | Guide how you work with data | Justify methodology choices |
| **Analytical** | Guide what you pay attention to | ONE focus per analysis section |
| **Interventional** | Guide recommendations | ONE concept per recommendation |
