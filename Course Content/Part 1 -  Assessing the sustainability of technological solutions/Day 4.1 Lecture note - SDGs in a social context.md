## Core Concepts and Definitions

### Sustainable Development (Brundtland 1987)
> **"Meeting the needs of the present without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs."**

This foundational definition emphasizes:
- Intergenerational equity
- The concept of "needs" (especially basic needs of the world's poor)
- Limitations imposed by technology and social organization on the environment's ability to meet present and future needs

### Wellbeing Economy
**Definition:** An economic system that enables current and future generations to live good lives within planetary boundaries.

**Key insight:** The wellbeing economy provides a **positive narrative** that the green transition is currently lacking - focusing on what we gain rather than what we must give up.

---

## Three Main Definitions of Wellbeing

| Approach | Core Principle | Implication for Sustainability |
|----------|---------------|-------------------------------|
| **Preferences** | More is always better | Problematic - drives unlimited consumption |
| **Psychological States** | Relative to others | Can lead to comparison-based consumption |
| **Theory of Human Needs** | Sufficiency and satiability | Most compatible with sustainability - needs can be met |

**Exam relevance:** The Theory of Human Needs approach is most compatible with sustainability because it suggests needs can be satisfied (satiability), whereas preference-based approaches assume infinite wants.

## The Doughnut Economics Model (Raworth 2017)

### Structure
The model visualizes sustainability as operating within a "safe and just space for humanity":

**Ecological Ceiling** (Outer boundary - Planetary Boundaries):
- Climate change
- Ocean acidification
- Chemical pollution
- Nitrogen & phosphorus loading
- Freshwater withdrawals
- Land conversion
- Biodiversity loss
- Air pollution
- Ozone layer depletion

**Social Foundation** (Inner boundary - Human Needs):
- Water
- Food
- Health
- Education
- Income & work
- Peace & justice
- Political voice
- Social equity
- Gender equality
- Housing
- Networks (connectivity)
- Energy

### Key Statistics from Quiz (Testable Facts)

| Issue | Statistic | Relevant SDGs |
|-------|-----------|---------------|
| Global undernourishment | **11%** of population | SDG 2, 3, 10 |
| Food waste globally | **33%** of food produced | SDG 2, 10, 12 |
| Earth's truly wild land remaining | Only **3%** | SDG 12, 15 |
| Planetary boundaries transgressed | **7 out of 9** | Multiple |
| CO2 per 100g protein (beef) | ~50 kg CO2-eq | SDG 2, 12, 13 |
| CO2 per 100g protein (tofu) | ~2 kg CO2-eq | SDG 2, 12, 13 |
| CO2 per 100g protein (nuts) | 0.26 kg CO2-eq | SDG 2, 12, 13 |

### Climate Impact by Wealth (Inequality Dimension)

| Population Segment | Share of Global GHG Emissions |
|-------------------|------------------------------|
| **Richest 10%** | 50% of emissions |
| **Middle 40%** | 43% of emissions |
| **Poorest 50%** | Only 8% of emissions |

**Key insight:** Climate change is deeply connected to inequality (SDG 10, 12, 13) - the wealthiest cause disproportionate environmental harm.

## Upstream vs. Downstream Thinking

### The Factory Analogy

**Upstream (Source/Prevention):**
- The economic system as a "factory" addicted to eternal GDP growth
- Some economic transactions cause more harm than good (like toxic waste)
- **Upstream solutions:** Change production methods to prevent problems at the source

**Downstream (Effects/Treatment):**
- Nature and society affected by economic decisions
- When people/animals "drink toxic water" they get sick
- A GDP-addicted economy leads to:
  - Transgression of planetary boundaries
  - Pressure on physical, mental, and social wellbeing
- **Downstream solutions:** Reactive, treat symptoms rather than causes

### Why Move Upstream?
| Approach | Characteristics |
|----------|----------------|
| **Upstream** | Proactive, focuses on prevention, addresses root causes |
| **Downstream** | Reactive, compartmentalized treatments, treats symptoms only |

## Lifecycle Thinking and Social Assessment

### Product Lifecycle Stages
1. **Extraction of raw materials**
2. **Production and installation**
3. **Use phase**
4. **End of life**

### Key Questions at Each Stage

**At each lifecycle stage, ask:**
- Is this meeting a **need** or a **luxury**?
- What is the **positive or negative impact**?
- **Who is impacted and how?**

### Social Impact Categories by Lifecycle Stage

#### Raw Material Extraction
- Forced labor (SDG 8, 16)
- Minimum wage issues (SDG 1, 8)
- Deprivation of liberty (SDG 16)
- Corruption (SDG 16)
- Environmental impacts on local communities (SDG 2, 6)

#### Production and Installation
- Safe working conditions (SDG 3, 8)
- Minimum wage (SDG 1, 8)
- Work injuries (SDG 3, 8)
- Political voice (SDG 16)
- Illness, wellbeing, stress (SDG 3)
- Local environment impacts (SDG 2, 6, 11)
- Job creation (SDG 1, 8)

#### Use Phase
- **Which human need is being met?**
- **How well is it being met?**

#### End of Life
- Working conditions (SDG 3)
- Toxin exposure (SDG 2, 3, 6, 12)

## Case Studies: Social Challenges in Value Chains

### Case 1: Cashew Nuts
**Location:** Primarily processed in India, Vietnam
**Social Issues:**
- Workers exposed to toxic cashew shell liquid
- Chemical burns and respiratory problems
- Low wages and poor working conditions
- Predominantly female workforce with limited rights

**Relevant SDGs:** 1 (Poverty), 3 (Health), 8 (Decent Work), 12 (Responsible Consumption)

### Case 2: Electric Vehicle Batteries (Cobalt Mining)
**Location:** Democratic Republic of Congo
**Social Issues:**
- Child labor in artisanal cobalt mines
- Dangerous working conditions
- Health hazards from toxic materials
- Poverty wages
- Human rights abuses

**Relevant SDGs:** 1, 3, 8, 12, 16

**Key insight:** Even "green" technologies can have significant negative social impacts in their value chains.

### Case 3: Cotton T-Shirt (Exercise Framework)
**Lifecycle Analysis:**

| Stage | Location | Potential Impacts |
|-------|----------|------------------|
| Raw materials (cotton) | Uzbekistan | Forced labor, water depletion, pesticide exposure |
| Production | Bangladesh | Poor working conditions, low wages, building safety |
| Use | Denmark | Consumer behavior, washing impacts |
| End of life | Denmark | Textile waste, recycling challenges |

## Connectivity as a Human Need

### Refugee Camp Example
**Question:** What is the most important need for people arriving in refugee camps?

**Answer:** **Connectivity** (SDG 9: Industry, Innovation and Infrastructure)

**Statistics:**
- 120 million refugees globally (2024)
- Up from 65 million (2015)

**Why connectivity matters:**
- Contact with family members
- Access to information
- Navigation and safety
- Access to services and aid
- Maintaining dignity and agency

**Key insight:** Human needs extend beyond basic physical necessities - social connection is fundamental.

## Key Tensions and Debates

### Achieving Sustainable Future: Two Approaches

| Approach | Focus | Critique |
|----------|-------|----------|
| **Reducing consumption & changing behavior** | Demand-side, sufficiency | May be politically difficult, requires social change |
| **Developing new technologies** | Supply-side, efficiency | Risk of rebound effects, doesn't address root causes |

**STS perspective:** Both approaches involve social choices and value judgments - technology alone is not neutral or sufficient.

### Social vs. Environmental Sustainability
- The lecture challenges the false dichotomy between social and environmental sustainability
- The Doughnut model shows they are **interconnected** - you need both
- Environmental degradation disproportionately affects vulnerable populations
- Social inequality drives unsustainable consumption patterns

## Summary: Key Takeaways for Exam

1. **Sustainable development** requires meeting present needs without compromising future generations (Brundtland 1987)

2. **Wellbeing economy** = enabling good lives within planetary boundaries

3. **Three definitions of wellbeing:** Preferences (more is better), Psychological states (relative), Human needs (satiable) - the needs approach is most compatible with sustainability

4. **Doughnut economics** provides a visual framework: stay within ecological ceiling while meeting social foundation

5. **Upstream thinking** addresses root causes; downstream thinking treats symptoms

6. **Lifecycle thinking** requires assessing social impacts at all stages: extraction, production, use, end-of-life

7. **Value chain analysis** reveals hidden social costs even in "green" products (e.g., EV batteries, cashews)

8. **Human needs** include connectivity and social dimensions, not just physical necessities

9. **Climate inequality:** Richest 10% cause 50% of emissions; poorest 50% cause only 8%

10. **SDGs are interconnected** - social and environmental sustainability cannot be separated
