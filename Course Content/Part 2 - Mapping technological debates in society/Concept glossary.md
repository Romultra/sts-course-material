## Introduction

### What is a Concept?

A **concept** is a **thinking tool**: an idea (often developed through empirical research) that helps you notice, describe, and reason about aspects of the world. Think of a concept as a *lens*: it makes you see some features while de-emphasizing others. Concepts do not automatically tell you what is "true," but they help you ask clearer questions and build more structured arguments.

### Three Types of Concepts

| Type | Purpose | Application |
|------|---------|-------------|
| **Methodological** | Guide how you work with data | Rules of thumb, quality criteria for analysis |
| **Analytical** | Guide what you pay attention to | Shape your analytical angle and questions |
| **Interventional** | Guide what you propose to do | Justify and communicate recommendations |

### Note on RRI and AREA

RRI and AREA are treated as **frameworks** (structured collections of concepts), not single concepts. In policy briefs, be precise and draw on the relevant *parts* rather than referring to "RRI" or "AREA" as a whole.

## Analytical Concepts

### 1. Normative Ethics

> *How can we judge whether a technology, design choice, or policy recommendation is morally good or right?*

**Definition:** The part of ethics asking what we *should* do, what is morally right, and what counts as a good society.

**Three Main Traditions:**
- **Consequentialism** - focus on outcomes
- **Deontology** - focus on duties/rights
- **Virtue ethics** - focus on character/virtues and relations

**Key Insight:** Different ethical traditions reach different conclusions not because people disagree on facts, but because each starts from different **baseline assumptions** about what matters morally.

**Application:** Helps identify the ethical "grammar" behind competing positions, clarify priorities, and reflect on limitations without reducing disagreements to ignorance or bad faith.

---

### 2. Consequentialist Ethics

> *Is a technology "good" because its outcomes are good? What counts as "utility"?*

**Definition:** Judges moral rightness of actions (or rules) only by their **outcomes**. An action is right insofar as it maximizes overall utility (welfare, happiness, wellbeing).

**Two Forms:**
- **Act consequentialism** - judge each act by its consequences
- **Rule consequentialism** - judge rules by whether they tend to produce best outcomes

**Strengths:** Offers measurement-heavy evaluation methods (e.g., lifecycle assessments)

**Blind Spots:** Rights, justice, autonomy, and structural inequality can be sidelined if hard to quantify

---

### 3. Deontological Ethics

> *Are there things we must never do - even if outcomes would be better?*

**Definition:** Grounds morality in **duties, rights, and principles** rather than outcomes.

**Key References:**
- **Kant's categorical imperative:**
  1. *Universality* - act only on maxims you could will as universal law
  2. *Humanity* - do not treat persons merely as means
- **Rawls' veil of ignorance:** Develops principles via hypothetical agreement, yielding equal liberties and justified inequalities only if they benefit the least advantaged

**Strengths:** Sensitizes analysis to rights-based critiques (privacy, autonomy, fairness)

**Limitations:** Can become rigid when duties collide

---

### 4. Virtue Ethics

> *Does a technology enable us to live a good life? How do we define that good life?*

**Definition:** Shifts moral evaluation from isolated acts/rules toward cultivation of **virtues and character** required for a good life. Virtues are developed through practice and reflection, often framed as balanced traits (e.g., courage between cowardice and recklessness).

**Related:** **Care ethics** - builds moral responsibility from relations of dependence, attentiveness to needs, and responsiveness; especially relevant where power asymmetries exist

**Key Question:** "What dispositions and professional responsibilities should guide design and assessment?"

**Strengths:** Strong for professional identity and relational responsibility

**Limitations:** Less direct as decision-procedure for large-scale societal trade-offs

---

### 5. Interpretative Flexibility

> *Is the technology "one thing," or does it become different things for different groups?*

**Definition:** Multiple social groups hold different (and sometimes conflicting) interpretations of what a technology is, what it means, or what it does. Differences appear in both design and use.

**Key Process:** **Closure** - over time, flexibility reduces when one interpretation stabilizes as dominant through social, cultural, and political processes (not just "better engineering")

**Importance:** Counters "technology is inevitable" narratives; steers attention to contestation, meaning-making, and stabilization

---

### 6. Script

> *What kind of user does a design assume? What behaviour does it invite, demand, or block?*

**Definition:** The set of assumptions and expectations **inscribed into a technical object** about users' competences, motives, environments, and "proper" ways of acting.

**Where Scripts Appear:** Interfaces, workflows, physical form, default settings, rules

**User Responses (Akrich's "de-scription"):**
- Follow
- Negotiate
- Repurpose
- Resist

**Key Insight:** Scripts make "technology is neutral" hard to sustain - design choices distribute agency and responsibility by shaping what is easy, hard, possible, or thinkable

---

### 7. Explicitly Political Technologies (Winner)

> *Who benefits - and who is excluded - because the artifact was designed that way?*

**Definition:** Artifacts/systems **deliberately designed** to produce or reinforce particular power relations (benefiting some groups while disadvantaging others).

**Key Point:** Politics is not an "after-effect" - it is part of the design and implementation

**Example:** Systems favouring large organizations over small ones

**Analysis Focus:** Design intent + politics: which interests shaped the artifact, and which social groups were made winners/losers?

---

### 8. Inherently Political Technologies (Winner)

> *What forms of governance does the technology require to function at all?*

**Definition:** Systems whose **technical requirements** make certain political arrangements more viable than others (e.g., centralization, hierarchy, security regimes).

**Key Point:** Politics may not depend on intent - the artifact's operational demands select for specific organizational forms

**Analysis Focus:** Institutional fit - when a technology scales, what kinds of authority structures, expertise regimes, and control infrastructures become "necessary"?

---

### 9. Paradigm and Thought Collective (Kuhn)

> *Why do some experts disagree so deeply about facts and what counts as evidence?*

**Paradigm:** A shared framework organizing what problems matter, what methods are legitimate, and what counts as evidence/explanation

**Thought Collective:** The group stabilized by shared assumptions and practices (including implicit norms, instruments, and standards)

**Normal Science:** Puzzle-solving within the paradigm

**Paradigm Shift:** Occurs when anomalies accumulate and cannot be contained, leading to crisis

**Key Insight:** Disagreements persist not because of "information deficits" but because of competing worlds of practice that structure observation, measurement, and credibility

---

### 10. Interactional Expertise and Trading Zone (Collins et al.)

> *How can people collaborate across disciplines without fully sharing methods or worldviews?*

**Trading Zone:** A space (institutional, practical, conceptual) where groups with different specialist languages coordinate by developing partial shared vocabularies, protocols, or "inter-languages"

**Interactional Expertise:** Fluency in a field's discourse and tacit standards *without* the ability to do its hands-on technical work - enabling translation, mediation, and negotiated alignment

**Reframes:** Interdisciplinary work as creating local coordination infrastructures (shared terms, boundary objects, routines) rather than expecting full consensus

---

### 11. Contributory vs. Interactional Expertise (Collins & Evans)

> *Who can do the work, and who can speak the language well enough to build bridges?*

| Type | Definition | Role |
|------|------------|------|
| **Contributory expertise** | Full mastery of domain's language AND practice | Produce specialized knowledge |
| **Interactional expertise** | Fluency in discourse and evaluative norms WITHOUT practical ability | Enable cross-domain cooperation and accountability |

**Key Insight:** Maps expertise claims without collapsing everything into "expert vs. lay"; highlights boundary-spanning roles

---

### 12. Performativity (of Markets)

> *Do economic models merely describe markets - or help make them?*

**Definition:** The thesis that economic theories, models, and tools can be **world-making**: they shape the practices, expectations, and infrastructures that constitute markets.

**Example:** The theory of supply and demand doesn't just explain market behaviour; it influences how market participants act

**Analytical Shift:** From "markets as natural mechanisms" to markets as **sociotechnical achievements** depending on tools, knowledge practices, and institutional scaffolding

---

### 13. Framing

> *What counts as "inside" the transaction - and what gets pushed "outside"?*

**Definition:** The process by which market actors define and delimit the **boundaries** of market transactions - determining what is exchanged and under what conditions.

**Example:** In organic produce markets, framing involves setting standards for what qualifies as "organic" and establishing certification

**Overflows:** Framings tend to produce unintended consequences that can spur public opposition and controversy, calling for re-framings

**Analysis Focus:** Boundary-making work - who defines relevant qualities/conditions, and what consequences are treated as out-of-frame?

---

### 14. Market Devices

> *How are market actions guided, coordinated, and made possible?*

**Definition:** Tools, technologies, and institutions that facilitate economic transactions (pricing mechanisms, quality standards, marketing strategies). Not 'pure' instruments but **material and discursive assemblages** that orient action.

**Key Point:** By structuring comparison, valuation, and exchange, market devices privilege certain forms of knowledge, value, and behaviour while marginalizing others

---

### 15. Market Socio-Technical Agencements (STAs) (Callon)

> *What assemblage of humans, technologies, rules, and infrastructures has to hold together for "the market" to exist?*

**Definition:** Arrangements and assemblages of human and non-human actors (including market devices) that constitute a market - institutions, technologies, regulations, cultural norms.

**Example:** The electric vehicle market is shaped by car manufacturers, government regulations, charging infrastructure, and consumer preferences

**Analytical Move:** "The market" becomes an empirical object to map: identify the agencement, then ask how it distributes agency, power, and responsibility

---

### 16. Users (Hyysalo, Jensen, Oudshoorn)

> *Who counts as a user and how do users reshape innovation rather than merely adopting it?*

**Definition:** Individuals or groups who interact with technology through consumption, adaptation, feedback, or participation in design.

**Key Insight (STS):** Users are **co-producers of innovation** - they:
- Modify technologies (user innovation)
- Develop new uses
- Build communities
- Enter design earlier through participatory/distributed innovation networks

**Disrupts:** Linear "producer --> user" models; makes diffusion, modification, and resistance part of innovation's core dynamics

---

### 17. Non-users (Wyatt) - Four Types

> *What does "non-use" do to a technology's trajectory and why is non-use meaningful?*

**Definition:** Those who do not engage with a technology, either voluntarily or involuntarily.

**Wyatt's Typology:**

| Type | Description |
|------|-------------|
| **Resisters** | Never use and actively choose not to |
| **Rejecters** | Used before, then stopped voluntarily |
| **Excluded** | Lack access due to socio-economic/infrastructural barriers |
| **Expelled** | Lose access involuntarily |

**Key Insight:** Non-use is not simply absence; it can be an active stance shaping public meanings, adoption pathways, and redesign pressures. Reveals hidden design assumptions ("the user as everybody")

---

### 18. Wicked Problems (Rittel & Webber)

> *How do you "solve" a societal problem when people disagree on what the problem is and the problem is evolving?*

**Tame Problems:** Well-defined, solvable in a rational/scientific manner; clear what counts as optimal solution

**Wicked Problems:** Ill-defined; no undisputed public good; no agreement on optimal outcome

#### Five Factors of Problem Wickedness:

| Factor | Description |
|--------|-------------|
| **No stopping rule** | Problem is ongoing; adapts to solutions or standards evolve |
| **Problem description determines solutions** | Different framings lead to different "good" solutions (framing controversy) |
| **Solutions are irreversible** | Path dependence makes experimentation high-stakes |
| **Better or worse, not true or false** | Solutions benefit some groups, disadvantage others |
| **Nested problems** | Problem partly caused by other wicked problems |

**Key Point:** Not "give up on solving" but replace expectation of optimal solutions with responsible approach: be explicit about assumptions; anticipate distributional consequences; treat solutions as interventions; expect disagreement as normal

---

### 19. Cultural Theory of Risk (Mary Douglas)

> *Why do some dangers become politicized "big risks" while others remain tolerated? Why do different people perceive the same risk differently?*

**Definition:** Treats risk not only as technical (probability x magnitude) but as something **interpreted, selected, and debated** within social groups.

**Key Elements:**
- People's risk judgements tied to trust/distrust in institutions
- Ideas about responsibility and blame
- Conflicts about legitimacy, justice, and decision rights

**Key Move:** Focus on **solidarities and attachments** - shared ways of life that shape what counts as acceptable vs. unacceptable

**Insight:** Public risk controversies are not "irrational perceptions" needing correction but arguments about desirable social order, burden distribution, and accountability

**Application:** Explains why actors disagree systematically (not randomly) and why "more facts" often don't end controversy

---

### 20. The Grid/Group Model (Mary Douglas)

> *How do we describe patterns in risk perceptions and preferences?*

**Two Dimensions:**
- **Group:** How strongly people are bound into collective identity and shared norms
- **Grid:** How strongly lives are structured by rules, roles, and hierarchies

**Four Stylized Positions (Ideal Types):**

| Position | Grid/Group | Characteristics |
|----------|------------|-----------------|
| **Hierarchists** | High group, high grid | Trust established authority; prefer regulation and expert management |
| **Egalitarians** | High group, low grid | Distrust hierarchy/markets; emphasize community control; often precautionary |
| **Individualists** | Low group, low grid | Prefer competition/markets; treat risk as opportunity; emphasize personal responsibility |
| **Fatalists** | Low group, high grid | Feel constrained but unrepresented; outcomes seem beyond control |

**Application:** Map predictable differences in risk rationalities - who trusts which institutions, who demands which forms of proof, and what governance arrangements are considered legitimate

## Methodological Concepts

### 21. Post Demographics (Rogers)

> *Is online friendship "real" friendship? Are social media accounts "real" people?*

**Definition (Richard Rogers):** A way to study personal data in social networking platforms, focusing on how profiling is performed and its consequences, departing from traditional demographic research.

**Key Takeaway:** We don't have to pretend a user account matches a person. We can study user accounts sociologically - studying statements for what they are (**traces**) without claiming they're "representative" of thoughts in the traditional sense.

---

### 22. Just Observe (Latour)

> *"Just observe and describe controversies"*

**Definition:** A methodological stance emphasizing careful, detailed observation of controversies as they unfold, **without imposing predetermined theoretical frameworks** or rushing to resolve disputes.

**The Challenge (Venturini):** Refrain from the urge to use your favorite theories, personal perspective, and own certainties.

**Venturini's Advice:**
- Do not restrain observation to any single theory or methodology
- Observe from as many viewpoints as possible
- Listen to actors' voices more than your own presumptions

**Reference:** Venturini, "Diving in Magma" (2011)

---

### 23. Second Degree Objectivity (Latour)

> *If there is no objectivity in controversies, how can they be studied objectively?*

**Answer:** We can be **objective about the dispute itself**.

**Definition:** Acknowledges the impossibility of first-degree objectivity (complete neutrality about what is controversial) while striving for rigorous analysis *of* the controversy.

**In Practice:** Being objective about what actors **say and do** regarding disputed facts, rather than trying to objectively determine which facts are true

**Reference:** Venturini, "Diving in Magma" (2011)

---

### 24. Exploratory Data Analysis (EDA)

> *Exploration finds questions; confirmation finds answers*

**Key Distinctions:**

| Exploratory | Confirmatory |
|-------------|--------------|
| Finds questions | Finds answers |
| Visualizing for yourself | Applying statistical toolbox to confirm hypotheses |

| Exploratory | Explanatory |
|-------------|-------------|
| Visualizing for yourself | Visualizing for others |
| Building internal knowledge | Communicating efficiently |
| Pivoting quickly between data facets | Requires different strategies |

---

### 25. Close / Distant Reading (Moretti)

> *Exploring data with algorithms and visualization is a form of reading*

**Franco Moretti's Distinction:**

| Close Reading | Distant Reading |
|---------------|-----------------|
| Traditional reading | Transforming source material |
| Source material not transformed | Making patterns appear |

**Application:** Data exploration qualifies as **distant reading** of what is said and disputed in the digital public space

**Reference:** Walsh & Horowitz, "Introduction to Text Analysis"

---

### 26. Quali-Quantitative Analysis

**Qualitative Analysis:**
- Focuses on interpretations and contexts
- Uses interviews, observations, long textual data
- Looks into richness and complexity through close reading

**Quantitative Analysis:**
- Focuses on measurements and statistical patterns
- Uses surveys, datasets, experimental data
- Identifies general trends and correlations (distant reading)

**Quali-Quantitative Analysis:**
- Combines both: preserves richness of qualitative insights while leveraging scalability of quantitative data
- Provides **thick description at scale**
- **Alternates** qualitative moments and quantitative moments (not simultaneous)

**Reference:** Venturini, "Diving in Magma" (2011)

---

### 27. Principle of Symmetry (Bloor)

> *Part of David Bloor's "strong programme" for sociology of scientific knowledge*

**Definition:** Demands that the **same types of explanations** are used for successful and unsuccessful knowledge claims alike.

**Application:** Methods should analyze "bad science" as well as "good science" - understand flat earthers' claims as well as renowned physicists' claims.

**Important:** This does NOT mean considering them the same (see Weighing Positions)

---

### 28. Actor (in Actor-Network Theory)

> *In ANT, an actor is someone or something that makes a difference to the situation*

**Definition:** Not every person is an actor - people who do not make a difference are not considered actors.

**Non-Human Actors:** Organizations, animals, rivers, technologies... can be actors insofar as they make a difference

**Course Application:** Interested in actors insofar as we can track and document which **difference(s)** they make - knowledge claims (involving expertise or risk) that influence decisions, answer questions, shape debate, mobilize new actors

**Reference:** Latour, "Where Are the Missing Masses?" (1992)

---

### 29. Follow the Actors (Latour)

> *We investigate according to actors' beliefs, not our own*

**Definition:** Listen to actors' knowledge claims and how they argue to retrace their underlying logic. Follow actors to uncomfortable places we disagree with and account for it.

**In Practice:**
- Pay special attention to specific terms and arguments used by different actors
- Do NOT replace their words with yours (even when you think they're the same)
- Especially relevant when using queries to investigate a dataset

**Reference:** Marres & Moats, "Mapping Controversies with Social Media" (2015)

---

### 30. Weighing Positions

> *Some actors are stronger and make a bigger difference than others. You must show it.*

**Key Distinction:**
- **Principle of symmetry:** Treat all actors equally, describe them fairly regardless of agreement
- **BUT:** This does NOT mean pretending they are equal

**Analytical Job:** Account for the fact that some actors make a bigger difference:
- Knowledge claims repeated by more actors
- Convinced more policymakers
- More influential on social media topics

**Method:** Following second-degree objectivity, provide an **objective description (measurement)** of each actor's ability to make a difference

---

### 31. Measurements

> *Making social life knowable, comparable, and actionable*

**Definition:** The practical work of making social life knowable within different research communities. Visualizations and numbers from datasets constitute measurements.

**Key Properties:**
- As **independent as possible** of who made it
- Same procedure should yield same result

**Post-Demographic Perspective:** Our corpus does not represent "the public opinion" - we neither measure nor aim to measure "what people say" in general. However, counting things in our dataset still constitutes measurements that can be compared, contrasted, and contextualized to generate valid insights.

---

### 32. Circulating Reference (Latour)

> *Scientific facts don't mirror reality directly but gain power through chains of transformations*

**Definition:** Facts gain their power to represent the world by moving through **chains of transformations** where each step changes the format but maintains the connection.

**Chain of Reference in Controversy Analysis:**
1. What people say online --> web documents to harvest
2. Web documents --> structured table of actor statements (with metadata)
3. Statements --> homogeneous, normalized statements
4. Normalized statements --> enriched data points (semantic map, clusters, issues coding)
5. Dataset --> measurements and visualizations (notebooks)
6. Visualizations --> arguments in policy brief

**Critical Point:** A single broken link (misusing visualization, failing to point to data) makes your entire argument fall apart

**Reference:** Latour, "Circulating Reference" in Pandora's Hope (1999)

---

### 33. Truth and Robustness

> *Arguments do not become robust because they are inherently true; they start becoming true as they get more robust*

**Key Insight:** Not enough to be convinced of validity - must demonstrate to others what makes reasoning **resilient to criticism and counterarguments**.

**Importance for Controversy Analysis:** Competing versions of "truth" emerge from disagreements. Must be capable of:
- Deconstructing others' arguments
- Examining foundations and assessing robustness
- Constructing compelling arguments of our own

**Solid Arguments:** Have specific structure that lets audience follow reasoning - see evidence used and understand how case is built

**Reference:** Latour, "Opening Pandora's Black Box" in Science in Action (1987)

---

### 34. The Toulmin Approach to Building an Argument

> *Stephen Toulmin (1958) - understanding how arguments are structured*

**Six Parts:**

| Component | Definition |
|-----------|------------|
| **Claim** | Statement that something is so |
| **Grounds** | Backing for claim: data, measurements |
| **Warrant** | Bridge-like statement explaining WHY claim follows from grounds |
| **Backing** | Support/explanation for the warrant |
| **Qualifier** | Degree of certainty offered (modality) |
| **Rebuttal** | Exceptions to the claim |

**Key Takeaway:** A robust argument is not just claim + grounds (data). More importantly, it's about the **relation** between grounds and claim (warrant and backing). To make better arguments, make warrant and backing **explicit**.

---

### 35. Technical Mediation

> *Technology is not neutral*

**Definition:** Technologies are better understood as **mediators** rather than mere intermediaries - they translate, modify, and redirect interactions.

**Double Implication:**
1. Technologies shape controversies by translating/modifying/redirecting
2. Tools we use (algorithms) to process data add mediations to our chain of reference

**Requirement:** When producing a visualization, be able to explain what is going on for each mediation involved

**Reference:** Latour, "On technical mediation" (1994)

---

### 36. Explanatory Visualization for Visual Arguments

> *Explaining requires different strategy than exploring - communicate context to audience*

**Key Principles:**
- Prioritize **clarity** above all else
- Minimize **distortions** (visualization is mediation - not neutral)

**Building Visual Arguments:**
- Unpack the mediation for the reader
- Highlight the visual pattern you ask reader to see
- Explicitly state WHY your reading follows from visual pattern

**Connects to Toulmin:** The warrant connecting ground to claim must be explicit

---

### 37. Annotation (of Data Visualizations)

> *Externalize internalized knowledge so audience has access to it*

**Purpose:** Guide reader's eyes to the visual pattern you need them to see

**Components of Good Visualization:**
- Title
- Legend
- Textual explanations
- **Annotations** (your best tool)

**Annotation Function:**
- Points to key visual pattern
- States what the pattern means
- Should be **in-place** (close to pattern itself)
- Text should state how interpretation follows from pattern (like Toulmin's warrant)

## Interventional Concepts

### 38. Public Engagement

> *How can research and technology development be shaped WITH the people who will live with its consequences?*

**Public Engagement with Science (PES):** Treats citizens and stakeholders as **legitimate participants** in dialogue, mutual learning, and decision-making.

**Contrast with Public Understanding of Science (PUS):**
- PUS linked to "deficit model" (resistance comes from lack of knowledge)
- PES recognizes controversies involve values, lived experience, and trust

**Formats:** Citizen panels, deliberative workshops, online forums, participatory research, co-design with users

**Risk:** **Tokenism** - engagement that looks participatory but doesn't share influence or change decisions

**Supports Recommendations Like:**
- "Include affected communities earlier"
- "Create a dialogue forum"
- "Co-design with users"

**Reference:** Stilgoe's text on RRI

---

### 39. Universal Design

> *Who gets excluded when we design for an "average user"?*

**Definition:** Designing products, services, and environments to be **inherently usable by as many people as possible**, regardless of age or ability - building inclusion into baseline design from the start.

**Contrast with "Accessibility":** Narrower practice of tailoring solutions for people with disabilities *after the fact*

**Seven Principles of Universal Design:**
1. Equitable use
2. Flexibility in use
3. Simple and intuitive use
4. Perceptible information
5. Tolerance for error
6. Low physical effort
7. Size and space for approach and use

**Key Argument:** Inclusion is not only a moral add-on but can **drive innovation** (designing for edge cases often produces better solutions for many)

**Supports Recommendations Like:**
- "Redesign interface to be intuitive under stress"
- "Add tolerance-for-error features"
- "Ensure multiple sensory channels for key information"

---

### 40. Social Acceptance

> *Why do some technologies succeed technically but fail socially?*

**Definition:** Willingness of individuals, communities, and stakeholders to support and integrate a technology into society.

**Multifaceted Nature:** Shaped by:
- Trust and fairness
- Emotions
- Perceived risks/benefits
- Norms
- Feeling involved in decisions

**Two Levels:**
- **Project/site-specific acceptance** (e.g., a local facility)
- **General acceptance** (support for technology in abstract)

**Heterogeneity:** Must account for differences across groups (age, gender, education, geography)

**Supports Recommendations About:**
- What to communicate
- Whom to involve
- What worries to address (safety, justice)
- How to make sense of resistance (vs. assuming "public irrationality")

---

### 41. Anticipation

> *How do we take responsibility when we cannot reliably predict what a technology will do?*

**Definition (RRI Framework):** Systematically asking "what if...?" questions to explore possible intended AND unintended impacts (social, ethical, environmental, political) - not only those fitting existing risk models.

**Key Feature:** Treats **uncertainty and ignorance as normal** conditions of innovation, not temporary lack of data

**Tools:** Foresight, technology assessment, horizon scanning, scenarios, vision assessment

**Tension:** "Prediction" can lock people into one imagined future; anticipation should **open up** multiple plausible futures and discuss what is desirable

**Supports Recommendations Like:**
- "Run scenario workshops"
- "Map second-order impacts"
- "Test how different futures affect different groups"

**Reference:** Stilgoe's text on RRI

---

### 42. Reflexivity

> *What assumptions are built into how we define the problem - and what do they make invisible?*

**Definition (Stilgoe et al.):** "Holding a mirror" up to one's own activities, commitments, framings, and limits of knowledge.

**Second-Order:** Questions not just methods/errors but also **values and theories** shaping research, innovation, and governance - recognizing different stakeholders may not share the same framing

**Supporting Practices:**
- Embedding social scientists/ethicists
- Multidisciplinary collaboration
- Ethical technology assessment
- Codes of conduct
- Structured reflection processes

**Key Point:** Reflexivity should not stay private inside labs; responsibility makes it a **public and institutional** matter

**Supports Recommendations Like:**
- "Make assumptions explicit"
- "Reframe the problem with stakeholders"
- "Audit which values the system encodes"

**Reference:** Stilgoe's text on RRI

---

### 43. Responsiveness

> *What does it mean to actually change course when concerns emerge?*

**Definition:** The capacity of research and innovation systems to **adjust direction** in response to new knowledge, shifting norms, and public/stakeholder values.

**Two Meanings of "Respond" (Pellizzoni):**
1. To **react**
2. To **answer**

**Enabling Mechanisms:**
- Standards and regulation
- Transparency/open access
- Stage-gates in R&D
- Moratoriums or precautionary pauses
- Value-sensitive design
- Policies keeping alternative pathways alive

**Critical Dependency:** Institutional culture and leadership - if participation happens but nothing can change, "engagement" becomes hollow

**Supports Recommendations Like:**
- "Build feedback loops into deployment"
- "Create criteria for pausing or redesign"
- "Set up governance that can revise goals"
- "Design for reversibility"

**Reference:** Stilgoe's text on RRI
