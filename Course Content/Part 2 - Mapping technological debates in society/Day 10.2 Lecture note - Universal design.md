# Day 10.2: Principles of Universal Design

**Guest Lecturer:** John Paulin Hansen (Professor, Psychologist, DTU Management)

**Core Question:** How can we design products and environments that are inherently accessible and usable by all people?

---

## Key Definitions

### Accessibility vs. Universal Design

| Concept | Definition |
|---------|------------|
| **Accessibility** | Tailors designs to meet the needs of individuals with disabilities, ensuring that products and environments are accessible to them |
| **Universal Design** | Focuses on creating products and environments that are **inherently accessible and usable by all people**, regardless of their abilities or age |

**Key distinction:** Accessibility retrofits solutions for specific groups; Universal Design builds inclusion into the design from the start.

---

## Why Universal Design Matters

### The Normal Distribution Problem
- Human characteristics (body size, vision, intelligence, etc.) follow a **normal distribution**
- Traditional design often targets only the "average" user (within 1 standard deviation)
- This excludes approximately **32% of the population** on the extremes

### The Impact of Age
- According to Cambridge University Inclusive Design Center:
  - By age 55-59, roughly **30%** of the population has less than "full ability"
  - By age 75-84, this rises to over **60%**
  - **Half the adult population** experiences reduced ability at some point

### The Psychological Cost of Exclusion
- **Social exclusion activates the same brain center as physical pain**
- Exclusion provokes:
  - Anxiety and uncertainty
  - Regret and hostility
  - Feeling of "inappropriateness"
  - Feeling of lack of control

### Connection to SDGs
- UN Sustainable Development Goals principle: **"LEAVING NO ONE BEHIND"**

## The Seven Principles of Universal Design

### 1. Equitable Use
> The design is useful and marketable to people with different abilities

**Example:** Level boarding on trains (benefits wheelchair users, people with strollers, luggage, etc.)

### 2. Flexibility in Use
> The design accommodates a wide range of individual preferences and abilities

**Examples:**
- Adjustable height desks (standing/sitting)
- Right and left-handed scissors

### 3. Simple and Intuitive Use
> Use of the design is easy to understand, regardless of the user's experience, knowledge, language skills, or current concentration level

**Examples:**
- IKEA assembly instructions (pictorial, no text)
- Airport wayfinding signage with symbols and multiple languages

### 4. Perceptible Information
> The design communicates necessary information effectively to the user, regardless of ambient conditions or the user's sensory abilities

**Example:** Restroom signs using both symbols and text, with accessibility indicators

### 5. Tolerance for Error
> The design minimizes hazards and the adverse consequences of accidental or unintended actions

**Examples:**
- "Undo" function in software
- Rumble strips on road edges to alert drifting drivers

### 6. Low Physical Effort
> The design can be used efficiently and comfortably and with a minimum of fatigue

**Examples:**
- Sinks at multiple heights for children and adults
- Automatic doors

### 7. Size and Space for Approach and Use
> Appropriate size and space is provided for approach, reach, manipulation, and use regardless of user's body size, posture or mobility

**Example:** Wide turnstiles in metro stations accommodating wheelchairs, strollers, and luggage

## Universal Design Driving Innovation

### Key Insight: Designing for Disability Creates Mainstream Products

| Innovation | Origin | Market Impact |
|------------|--------|---------------|
| **Electric Toothbrush** | Created by Dr. Philippe Guy Woog (1954) for patients with limited motor skills | Global market valued at $2,200M (2016), projected $3,770M by 2023 |
| **Dropped Kerb (Curb Cut)** | Selwyn Goldsmith, *Designing for the Disabled* (1963) | Now standard infrastructure worldwide |

### Hearing Aids: A Case Study in Evolution
- **Early days:** Ear trumpets, bulky body-worn devices (1940s)
- **1950s:** Clip-on and pocket devices
- **1960s:** Hearing glasses, in-ear devices (Danish company Danavox)
- **Today:** Nearly invisible devices, multiple form factors
- **Tomorrow:** Apple AirPods Pro with hearing aid features and real-time translation

**Market Data:**
- 16 million units sold worldwide annually
- Only 1 in 3 people who need hearing aids use them
- Untreated hearing loss costs EU **185 billion EUR annually**
- Danish companies (WDH, GN, Widex) hold ~48% of global market

## DTU Case Studies

### DrinkSaver (BEVICA)
- **Origin:** DTU Design & Innovation semester project
- **Purpose:** Plastic lid that prevents drink spillage
- **Primary Users:** People with motor disabilities, wheelchair users
- **Developed with:** Roskilde Festival
- **Broader Adoption:** Sailors, toddlers, anyone wanting spill protection
- **Recognition:** Red Dot Design Award Winner 2024

**Lesson:** Designing for extreme users creates products with universal appeal.

### GazeTalk
- Eye-tracking communication system for users with severe motor disabilities
- Enables typing and communication through eye movement alone
- Example user: Birger Bergman Jeppesen

## Connection to RRI Framework

Universal Design operationalizes **Responsible Research and Innovation (RRI)** through:

| RRI Dimension | Universal Design Application |
|---------------|------------------------------|
| **Anticipation** | Considering diverse user needs before design decisions are finalized |
| **Reflexivity** | Examining assumptions about "normal" users |
| **Inclusion** | Involving users with diverse abilities in the design process |
| **Responsiveness** | Adapting designs based on feedback from marginalized users |

## Key Takeaways for Exam

1. **Universal Design** is proactive (built-in from the start) vs. **Accessibility** which is often reactive (retrofitted)

2. **Seven Principles** provide a practical framework for inclusive design evaluation

3. **Designing for the margins** often creates better products for everyone (curb cuts, electric toothbrushes)

4. **Exclusion has real psychological costs** - activates same brain regions as physical pain

5. Universal Design is a **practical implementation of RRI's inclusion dimension**

6. The aging population makes Universal Design increasingly important (half of adults will experience reduced ability)
