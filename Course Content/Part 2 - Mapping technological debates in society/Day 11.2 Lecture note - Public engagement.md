# Day 11.2: Public Engagement with Science and Technology

## Overview

This lecture introduces different approaches to involving stakeholders in technology development, focusing on the evolution from **public understanding** to **public engagement** and **citizen science**. It examines the democratic value of public participation and distinguishes between **upstream** and **downstream** engagement.

---

## The Core Problem

Technological projects frequently meet resistance because:
- Stakeholders may lack literacy about the technology
- Stakeholders may have **legitimate concerns** that are not addressed
- Stakeholders and their concerns only become clear once the project is mature
- By that point, decisions are already **locked-in**

**Key question:** How, when, and why should we engage whom in technology development?

---

## The Information Deficit Hypothesis

A foundational (but limited) assumption about public resistance to technology:

| Assumption | Implication |
|------------|-------------|
| Public acceptance depends on the public's **level of literacy** | If people understood the science, they would accept it |
| Lack of literacy can be fixed by **communicating information from experts to the public** | One-way communication from experts to laypeople |

**Critique:** This model assumes the public is simply ignorant and ignores legitimate concerns, values, and forms of lay expertise.

---

## Historical Evolution: From Understanding to Engagement

### 1980s: Public Understanding of Science (PUS)

- Experts need to explain their knowledge better
- Communication should make sense to people's **everyday problems**
- Still primarily **one-way communication** (expert to public)
- The public must be made **literate**

### 1990s: Public Engagement with Science (PES)

- The public needs to be **involved in the production of knowledge**
- This is where decisions are taken and courses of action enabled
- Shift toward **dialogue and participation**
- Influenced by cases like AIDS activists transforming biomedical research (see Epstein's *Impure Science*)

---

## Three Models of Public Participation

Based on Callon's (1999) framework for understanding lay people in knowledge production:

### Model 1: Public Education Model

| Feature | Description |
|---------|-------------|
| Knowledge relationship | Scientific and lay knowledge are **mutually exclusive** |
| Authority | Scientific knowledge draws authority from **institutional trust** |
| Public role | **Passive recipients** of information |
| Goal | **The public must be made literate** |
| Example | World Science Festival - cultivating a public "informed by science, inspired by its wonder" |

### Model 2: Public Consultation Model

| Feature | Description |
|---------|-------------|
| Knowledge relationship | Scientific knowledge is **incomplete** |
| Lay knowledge value | Specific forms can **complete the picture** |
| Public role | **Consulted stakeholders** |
| Goal | **The public must be consulted** |
| Concern focus | Knowledge production should address concerns of **those affected** |
| Example | NASA Airathon - public develops algorithms using NASA data |

### Model 3: Co-creation of Knowledge Model

| Feature | Description |
|---------|-------------|
| Knowledge relationship | The separation between lay and expert knowledge **advantages some, disadvantages others** |
| Timing | It often only becomes evident **post facto** who profits and who does not |
| Public role | **Active participants** in knowledge production |
| Goal | **The public must be involved** |
| Example | eBird - citizen science where public contributes data for conservation research |

---

## Key Characteristics of Public Engagement in STS

Public engagement (in the STS sense) emphasizes:

- **Dialogue and participation**, not one-way communication
- Two-way learning process

### Key Aims

1. Connect engineering decisions with **societal values and concerns**
2. Include **non-expert perspectives** in technological development
3. Increase **trust, legitimacy, and social responsibility**

### Success Indicators

- **Meaningful public involvement** (not token participation)
- **Mutual learning** between engineers and society
- Public input **influences real outcomes**

## Approaches for Public Engagement and Co-creation

| Approach | Key Features | Source |
|----------|--------------|--------|
| **Anticipatory governance** | Proactive governance of emerging technologies | Barben et al. 2008 |
| **Real-time Technology Assessment** | Ongoing assessment during development | Guston and Sarewitz 2002 |
| **Constructive Technology Assessment (CTA)** | Dialogue among and early interaction with new actors | Schot and Rip 1997 |
| **Value-sensitive design** | Design of technology that accounts for human values | Friedman 1996 |
| **Open innovation** | External ideas and paths to market | Chesbrough 2023 |
| **Midstream modulation** | Influencing R&D decisions during development | Fisher et al. 2006 |
| **Public dialogue methods** | Consensus conferences, citizens' juries, deliberative mapping, deliberative polling, focus groups | Chilvers 2010 |

---

## Upstream vs. Downstream Engagement

| Type | Timing | Characteristics | Impact Potential |
|------|--------|-----------------|------------------|
| **Upstream** | Early in development | Before major decisions are locked-in | Higher - can shape direction |
| **Downstream** | Late in development | After technology is largely developed | Lower - mainly acceptance/rejection |

**Key insight:** Involving society **early** can improve innovation quality and overall social support.

---

## Case Study: Foldit - Successful Public Engagement

**Foldit** is an online game where the public helps solve protein-folding challenges.

### Why It Works

- Engages **non-experts** in real scientific problem-solving
- Combines **human intuition** with computational engineering
- Public contributions led to **published scientific breakthroughs**

### Key Takeaway

Involving society early can:
- Improve **innovation quality**
- Increase **overall social support**

## Examples of Public Engagement Initiatives

### Example 1: World Science Festival
- **Type:** Public education model
- **Goal:** Cultivate a general public "informed by science, inspired by its wonder, convinced of its value"
- **Format:** Multimedia content spanning theatre, film, dance, music, live experiences, discussions

### Example 2: NASA Airathon
- **Type:** Public consultation / crowdsourcing
- **Goal:** Develop algorithms for estimating daily air pollutant levels
- **Outcome:** 1,200+ submissions from 1,000+ participants; $25,000 in prizes
- **Data used:** NASA satellite data, model outputs, ground measurements

### Example 3: eBird (Cornell Lab of Ornithology)
- **Type:** Co-creation / citizen science
- **Goal:** Advance science and conservation through bird sightings
- **Outcome:** Contributions to hundreds of conservation decisions and peer-reviewed papers
- **Scale:** World's largest birding community

---

## Analyzing Public Engagement Initiatives

When evaluating any public engagement initiative, consider:

### Questions to Ask

1. **Who initiated it, for whom, and why?**
   - What are the underlying motivations?
   - Who benefits?

2. **How effective was the initiative?**
   - Did public input influence real outcomes?
   - Was there mutual learning?

3. **What limitations exist?**
   - Accessibility barriers
   - Representation gaps
   - Power imbalances

## Key Terms for Exam

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Information deficit hypothesis** | The assumption that public resistance stems from lack of knowledge, fixable by better communication |
| **Public Understanding of Science (PUS)** | 1980s approach focused on making science accessible to laypeople |
| **Public Engagement with Science (PES)** | 1990s shift toward dialogue and public involvement in knowledge production |
| **Lay expertise** | Knowledge held by non-professionals based on lived experience |
| **Upstream engagement** | Public involvement early in technology development before decisions are locked-in |
| **Downstream engagement** | Public involvement late in development, often focused on acceptance |
| **Co-creation** | Collaborative knowledge production involving both experts and publics |
| **Citizen science** | Scientific research conducted with participation from the general public |
| **Constructive Technology Assessment** | Approach emphasizing early dialogue with diverse actors during development |
| **Value-sensitive design** | Design approach that accounts for human values throughout the process |

---

## Summary

1. **Public resistance** to technology is not simply about lack of knowledge - people may have legitimate concerns
2. The field has evolved from **understanding** (educate the public) to **engagement** (involve the public)
3. Three models exist: **education**, **consultation**, and **co-creation** - each with different assumptions about lay knowledge
4. **Upstream engagement** (early involvement) is more effective than downstream
5. Successful engagement requires **meaningful participation**, **mutual learning**, and **real influence** on outcomes
6. Multiple methods exist for public engagement, from consensus conferences to citizen science platforms
