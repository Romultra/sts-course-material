## The AREA Framework for RRI

**Responsible Research and Innovation (RRI)** is operationalized through the **AREA framework**:
- **A**nticipate
- **R**eflect
- **E**ngage
- **A**ct

> Originally developed as "Anticipation, Reflexivity, Inclusion, and Responsiveness" by **Stilgoe, Owen, and Macnaghten (2013)** in foundational research policy work. Later adopted by the UK **Engineering and Physical Sciences Research Council (EPSRC)**.

---

## The Four Dimensions of RRI

### 1. Anticipate

**Core principle:** Take uncertainty seriously - it is hard to predict the effects of emerging technologies.

**Goal:** Explore and imagine as many **possible** intended and unintended consequences as possible.

**Key distinction:**
- **NOT** establishing probability of only well-understood consequences
- **BUT** expanding imagination beyond predictable outcomes

**Common scientific impulse:** Narrow imagination to what falls within area of expertise.
- Anticipation must **work against this impulse**

**Comfortable questions (within expertise):**
- Do we have appropriate methodology?
- Risk assessment in place?
- Data management plan?
- Lab health and safety?
- Ethical approval and informed consent?

**Expanded anticipation questions:**
- Is this **socially desirable**?
- For **which stakeholders**?
- In **what scenarios**?
- Addressing **what kind of larger societal challenges**?

---

### 2. Reflect

**Core principle:** Science is already good at organized scepticism (Popper), but RRI requires a specific type of reflection.

**RRI reflection focuses on:**
- Consequences of **own assumptions**
- The way we have **framed the problem**
- Our **motivations** for doing the research

**Key reflective questions:**
- Which groups or individuals have a **stake** in the research and its outcomes?
- Which groups or individuals **influence** the research? How do they bring their influence to bear?
- Which groups or individuals can be held **responsible** for unwanted/harmful consequences?

**Meta-questions for deeper reflection:**
- How do you know the answers to these questions?
- Who decides who the right stakeholders are?
- How do we decide what kind of stakes they have?
- Who should be held accountable?

---

### 3. Engage

**Core principle:** Invite societal stakeholders to participate in your anticipation and reflection.

**Questions to explore with stakeholders:**
- Do they begin from the **same assumptions** as you?
- Do they see the **same purpose and motivation** for the research?
- Do they **frame the problems** the same way?
- Do they agree on **who has a stake**?
- Do they agree on **who should be held accountable**?
- Do they agree on **who has influence** on the research?
- Can they imagine **other consequences** (intended or unintended)?
- Can they imagine **other scenarios**?
- Do they agree on what is **socially acceptable/desirable**?

---

### 4. Act

**Core question:** How do we translate anticipation, reflection, and engagement into practice?

**Requirements for action:**

1. **Interdisciplinary training and collaboration**
   - The rationale behind this course

2. **Methods for technology assessment:**
   - **Part 1:** Conduct sustainability assessment
   - **Part 2:** Map and analyse public controversies around technology and innovation
   - **Part 2:** Organize public engagement exercises
   - **Part 2:** Apply design methods with diverse stakeholders in mind

3. **Creating structural incentives**
   - Integration of RRI principles into research funding systems

## The Concept Glossary: Three Types of Concepts

The Concept Glossary is a **learning resource** containing all concepts from Part 2.

> A **concept** is a thinking tool: an idea that helps you notice, describe, and reason about aspects of the world. Think of it as a lens - it makes you see some features while de-emphasizing others.

### 1. Methodological Concepts

**Purpose:** Guide how you work with data

**Application:** Parse and analyse a dataset of statements following controversy mapping approach

**Function:** Provide rules of thumb and quality criteria:
- What to look for
- What to ignore
- How to justify choices
- How to make analysis robust

### 2. Analytical Concepts

**Purpose:** Guide what you pay attention to in the phenomenon you study

**Application:** Formulate questions and make explicit which dimensions you foreground

**Function:** Shape your particular analytical angle

**Rule:** Use **ONE analytical focus** per analysis section (e.g., ethics, markets, or risk perception)

### 3. Interventional Concepts

**Purpose:** Guide what you propose to do based on the analysis

**Application:** Develop, justify, and communicate recommendations in policy brief

**Function:** Help respond to issues identified in analysis

**Rule:** Use **ONE concept** per recommendation

## RRI and the Policy Brief

### Structure of the Policy Brief

1. **Client identification**
   - Decision maker who will implement your proposed sustainability improvements
   - Gives focus for your policy brief
   - Proposal can come from Assignment B (Part 1)

2. **Objective**
   - Help client understand the energy island controversy
   - Identify actors and their concerns
   - Develop an RRI strategy for your proposal

### The RRI Logic for Policy Briefs

> RRI is about **anticipating how your innovation can be a problem in society**. To do so, you **reflect on the limits of your own assumptions** by **engaging stakeholders who see it differently**.

### Required Components

| Section | Approach | Purpose |
|---------|----------|---------|
| **Methodology** | Quantitatively map actors and issues using controversy mapping methods | Show client how you chose to focus on certain actor statements and why |
| **Analysis** | Qualitatively analyse actor statements using Part 2 analytical concepts | Help client understand what actors are discussing (risk perceptions, ethical frameworks, ideas about expertise, users, markets, etc.) |
| **Recommendation** | Suggest RRI strategy based on findings | Identify who could be engaged, how to reflect on assumptions, how to anticipate adverse consequences |

### Recommendation Requirements

Your RRI strategy recommendation should:
- Be based on your controversy mapping findings
- Identify **who** could be engaged
- Specify **what way** to help reflect on assumptions
- Anticipate **what kind** of adverse consequences
- Be justified by **one of the four AREA dimensions**

1. **AREA Framework:** Memorize all four dimensions and their key questions
   - Anticipate: Explore possible impacts beyond what's predictable
   - Reflect: Examine own assumptions, framings, motivations
   - Engage: Include stakeholders in anticipation and reflection
   - Act: Implement through methods, training, and structural incentives

3. **Part 1 to Part 2 connection:** Sustainability assessment provides the proposal; controversy mapping and RRI provide the strategy for responsible implementation

4. **Policy brief logic:** Map controversy quantitatively, analyse qualitatively, recommend based on RRI dimensions

5. **Stilgoe et al. (2013):** Foundational reference for RRI framework

## Summary: The RRI Process

```
ANTICIPATE possible consequences (expand beyond expertise)
        |
        v
REFLECT on assumptions, framings, motivations
        |
        v
ENGAGE stakeholders to challenge your perspective
        |
        v
ACT by changing direction/trajectory of research and innovation
```

**The core insight:** RRI is not about predicting the future with certainty, but about **opening up imagination** through **stakeholder engagement** and **honest reflection** on the limits of your own perspective.
