**R**esponsible **R**esearch and **I**nnovation (RRI) is a widely applied approach to ensure the societal acceptability and desirability of science and technology. Over the past 15 years, it has been adopted by major funding bodies and engineering associations as part of their assessment criteria and ethical standards. We will work with the **AREA framework**, a specific implementation of RRI developed in the UK by the Environmental and Physical Sciences Research Council.



Whereas Part 1 taught you how to objectively assess the various sustainability tradeoffs of engineering solutions, **it is obviously controversial what counts as 'desirable' or 'acceptable' solutions** to different groups in society with different values, different problem definitions, and different things at stake. RRI takes that into account by insisting that responsibility is not only about anticipating possible harms and evaluating tradeoffs in advance, but about **opening those discussions up to relevant societal stakeholders with different priorities and perspectives** and doing so continuously.

How is that possible? This is essentially the focus of the entire Part 2 of the course, but we begin with RRI as an overall framework. This is also the focus of your exam assignment, which asks you to recommend strategies to improve RRI based on a mapping of the controversy over the Danish energy islands.

This lecture thus gives you **the conceptual basis for why we are doing what we are doing in Part 2**, how the subsequent lectures support those overall goals, and how you should approach RRI in your exam assignment.

As a precursor to those discussions we will revisit the notion of **technological wicked problems** that we briefly touched upon in the general introduction on the first day of the course. RRI builds on decades of research in the sociology of science and technology, research policy, and related fields, wicked problems being part of that.
