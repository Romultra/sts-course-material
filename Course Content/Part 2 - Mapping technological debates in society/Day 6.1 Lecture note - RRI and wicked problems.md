## Part 1: Wicked Problems

### Definition and Origin

**Key Scholars:** Horst Rittel & Melvin Webber (1973) - "Dilemmas in a general theory of planning"

> "The search for scientific bases for confronting problems of social policy is bound to fail, because of the nature of these problems. They are 'wicked' problems, whereas science has developed to deal with 'tame' problems."

**Core Insight:**
- In a pluralistic society, there is **no undisputable public good**
- There is **no objective definition of equity**
- It makes **no sense to talk about 'optimal solutions'** to social problems unless severe qualifications are imposed
- There are no "solutions" in the sense of **definitive and objective answers**

### Wicked vs. Tame Problems

| Tame Problems | Wicked Problems |
|---------------|-----------------|
| Well-defined | Ill-defined |
| Clear stopping point | No stopping rule |
| Solutions are true/false | Solutions are better/worse |
| Can be tested experimentally | Irreversible interventions |
| Isolated from other problems | Symptom of other problems |

---

### Five Key Characteristics of Wicked Problems

#### 1. No Stopping Rule - Problems Are Ongoing

**Key Points:**
- Solutions temporarily stabilize situations, but underlying complexity means problems evolve
- You can always imagine an even better state
- Solutions are **interventions** that affect the field and change the nature of the problem
- Solutions have **unintended consequences**

**Examples:**
- **Poverty alleviation:** May temporarily work, but causes of poverty constantly change, destabilizing solutions. What does "end to poverty" even look like?
- **Crime reduction:** Efforts affect criminal behavior - they adapt and develop new strategies, changing the nature of the problem

---

#### 2. Solutions Are Irreversible - Little Room to Experiment

**Key Points:**
- Solutions create **path dependence** - once implemented, they constrain future options
- Hard to learn through low-cost trial-and-error
- Can be one-shot, high-stakes operations

**Examples:**
- **Electric cars:** Require massive investment in charging infrastructure, making it difficult to experiment with alternative solutions
- **Nuclear reactors:** Consequences of failure could be catastrophic, with effects beyond just not solving climate crisis

---

#### 3. Problem Description Determines Possible Solutions

**Key Points:**
- Tame problems have objective answers because they are well-defined
- Wicked problems are **ill-defined** and open to interpretation
- Multiple groups with different **values**, **stakes**, and **problem definitions** create **framing controversies**
- Different worldviews lead to different relevant solutions and different experts needed

**Example:**
- **Circumcision for infant boys:** Is this a religious question? A medical question about pain and health? A legal question about parental vs. child rights? The framing determines which solutions are even considered.

---

#### 4. Problems Are Symptoms of Other Wicked Problems

**Key Points:**
- Difficult to **delimit** wicked problems from each other
- Adds to complexity
- Requires being explicit about assumptions and framings

**Example:**
- **Unemployment and AI development** are now interdependent wicked problems - addressing one affects the other

---

#### 5. Solutions Are Better or Worse, Not True or False

**Key Points:**
- Due to differing interests, solutions benefit some groups while disadvantaging others
- Best approach: Be **explicit about who benefits and how**

**Example:**
- **Urban gentrification:**
  - Good for: Property owners (prices rise), municipality (tax revenue), local businesses (buying power increases)
  - Bad for: Citizens who benefited from cheap housing and less regulated urban spaces

## Part 2: Responsible Research and Innovation (RRI)

### Why We Need RRI: The Consequence of Wicked Problems

> Wicked problems require **the responsible engineer** - someone who can navigate complexity, uncertainty, and competing stakeholder interests.

---

### A Brief Prehistory of RRI

**Historical Context:**
- History shows many technologies promised progress but had **adverse consequences**: Asbestos, PCBs, Social Media
- **Francis Bacon (17th c.):** Science "for the relief of man's estate"
- **J.D. Bernal (1939):** Science in the service of society
- **Vannevar Bush (1945):** The endless frontier of science

**Evolution:**
- **1980s onwards:** Societal push for science communication, public engagement, mission-driven research
- **Late 20th century:** Ideas pioneered by researchers in natural/technical sciences concerned about **dual use** and consequences (synthetic biology, nuclear physics, AI)
- **Early 2000s:** Formally called "RRI"

**Notable Case:** SPICE Project (Stratospheric Particle Injection for Climate Engineering) - an example of emerging technology requiring responsible governance

---

### The AREA Framework for RRI

**Developed by:** UK Engineering and Physical Sciences Research Council (EPSRC)
**Based on:** Foundational work by Jack Stilgoe, Richard Owen, and Phil Macnaghten (late 2000s)
**Original terms (Stilgoe et al., 2013):** Anticipation, Reflexivity, Inclusion, Responsiveness

| Dimension | Description |
|-----------|-------------|
| **A**nticipate | Explore possible impacts (intended and unintended) |
| **R**eflect | Consider purpose, motivations, assumptions, framings |
| **E**ngage | Open discussions to relevant societal stakeholders |
| **A**ct | Use these processes to influence research direction |

---

### Anticipate

**Core Principle:** Take uncertainty seriously - it is hard to predict effects of emerging technologies

**Goal:** Explore/imagine as many **possible** intended and unintended consequences as possible
**NOT:** Establish certainty about probability of well-understood consequences only

**Challenge:** Typical scientific impulse is to narrow imagination to impacts within area of expertise. Anticipation must **work against that impulse**.

**Comfortable questions (within expertise):**
- Do we have appropriate methodology?
- Risk assessment?
- Data management plan?
- Lab health and safety?
- Ethical approval?
- Informed consent?

**Expanded questions (RRI approach):**
- Is this socially desirable?
- For which stakeholders?
- In what scenarios?
- Addressing what kind of larger societal challenges?

---

### Reflect

**Core Principle:** Science is already good at reflecting (organized scepticism - Popper), but RRI requires broader reflection

**Focus Areas:**
- Consequences of own **assumptions**
- How we have chosen to **frame the problem**
- Our **motivations** for doing the research

**Key Questions:**
- Which groups/individuals have a **stake** in the research and its outcomes?
- Which groups/individuals **influence** the research? How?
- Which groups/individuals can be held **responsible** for unwanted/harmful consequences?

**Meta-Questions:**
- How do you know the answers to these questions?
- Who decides who the right stakeholders are?
- How do we decide what kind of stakes they have?
- Who should be held accountable?

---

### Engage

**Core Principle:** Invite societal stakeholders to participate in your anticipation and reflection

**Questions to Explore with Stakeholders:**
- Do they share your **assumptions**?
- Do they see the same **purpose and motivation** for the research?
- Do they **frame the problem** the same way?
- Do they agree on **who has a stake**?
- Do they agree on **who should be accountable**?
- Do they agree on **who has influence**?
- Can they imagine **other consequences or scenarios**?
- Do they agree on what is **socially acceptable/desirable**?

---

### Act

**Core Question:** How do we implement RRI in practice?

**Requirements:**

1. **Interdisciplinary training and collaboration**
   - Why courses like STS exist

2. **Methods and tools:**
   - Technology assessment
   - Scenario scanning
   - Public engagement and deliberation
   - Co-design

3. **Course-specific skills:**
   - Conduct sustainability assessment (Part 1)
   - Map and analyse public controversies around technology (Part 2)
   - Organize public engagement exercises (Part 2)
   - Apply design methods with diverse stakeholders in mind (Part 2)

4. **Structural incentives:**
   - Funding systems incorporating RRI principles

---

### RRI in Practice: Real-World Requirements

| Organization | RRI Implementation |
|--------------|-------------------|
| **European Commission H2020** (2014-2020) | Explicit RRI requirement for EUR 80 billion program |
| **Horizon Europe** | EUR 95 billion successor integrating RRI principles |
| **UK EPSRC** | Actively promotes AREA framework |
| **Innovation Fund Denmark** | Implements EC's RRI definition in evaluation criteria |

**IEEE Code of Ethics - RRI-aligned commitments:**
- Improve "societal implications of conventional and emerging technologies"
- "Comply with ethical design and sustainable development practices"
- "Hold paramount the safety, health, and welfare of the public"
- "Avoid conflicts of interest whenever possible, and disclose them when they exist"

## Key Connections for Exam Preparation

### How RRI Addresses Wicked Problems

| Wicked Problem Characteristic | RRI Response |
|------------------------------|--------------|
| No stopping rule | Ongoing **anticipation** and monitoring |
| Irreversible solutions | Careful **reflection** before acting |
| Problem framing matters | **Engage** diverse stakeholders to surface different frames |
| Interconnected problems | **Reflect** on broader system implications |
| No true/false solutions | **Act** transparently about trade-offs and beneficiaries |

## Summary: The Responsible Engineer

The responsible engineer:
1. **Recognizes** wicked problems and their characteristics
2. **Anticipates** possible impacts beyond their expertise
3. **Reflects** on assumptions, framings, and motivations
4. **Engages** diverse stakeholders in deliberation
5. **Acts** to influence research/innovation direction based on these processes
