# Day 6.2 - Ethics

---

**Normative ethics** explores what makes something morally right or good. It follows three main traditions: **consequentialism**, **deontology**, and **virtue ethics**. Each tradition has distinct assumptions about moral goodness, orienting our thinking differently. For instance, deontology emphasizes morally good *intentions* and adherence to *universal norms*, while consequentialism disregards intentions, focusing exclusively on *outcomes*. Care ethics shifts focus again, emphasizing that morality arises from caring *relationships* rather than isolated actions or individuals. Different ethical theories thus lead to varied conclusions about whether a technology is morally good or bad, not necessarily because they disagree on outcomes, but because their base assumptions and thus their reasoning differs.



Why learn multiple ethical theories? Rather than providing absolute moral judgments, these theories teach us how to evaluate and critically reflect on why, given certain assumptions, we judge technologies as good or bad. Understanding various theories helps analyse how certain evaluative methods reach specific conclusions. For example, lifecycle assessment, which you learnt about in the first part of the course, uses a utilitarian framework that measures and quantifies consequences. From a deontological or virtue ethics perspective, however, this approach may seem limited. Examining lifecycle assessment through multiple ethical lenses highlights its potential blind spots and limitations. As responsible engineers, you thus need to learn how to juggle multiple ethical perspectives simultaneously, not relying on any single theory alone.
