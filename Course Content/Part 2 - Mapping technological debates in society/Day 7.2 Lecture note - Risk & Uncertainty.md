## Key Concepts Overview

### Risk Assessment Matrix

Traditional risk assessment combines two dimensions:

| Likelihood / Severity | Catastrophic | Critical | Marginal | Negligible |
|----------------------|--------------|----------|----------|------------|
| **Frequent** | High | High | Serious | Medium |
| **Probable** | High | High | Serious | Medium |
| **Occasional** | High | Serious | Serious | Low |
| **Remote** | Serious | Medium | Medium | Low |
| **Improbable** | Medium | Medium | Medium | Low |
| **Eliminated** | Eliminated | Eliminated | Eliminated | Eliminated |

**Key insight:** Risk = Likelihood x Severity of adverse consequences

## Known Unknowns vs. Unknown Unknowns

Two important categories of uncertainty in risk management:

| Category | Definition | Source |
|----------|------------|--------|
| **Known Unknowns** | Risks we are aware of but cannot precisely quantify | John Adams, "Risk" (1995) |
| **Unknown Unknowns** | Risks we are not even aware exist | Donald Rumsfeld (2002) |

**Relevance to RRI:** Anticipating both intended and unintended consequences requires acknowledging different types of uncertainty.

## The Cultural Theory of Risk

### Origins and Key Scholar

- **Developed by:** Mary Douglas (1980s)
- **Academic tradition:** Functionalist anthropology
- **Key work:** "Risk and Culture: An Essay on the Selection of Technological and Environmental Dangers" (with Aaron Wildavsky)
- **Review article:** Tansey, J., & O'riordan, T. (1999). "Cultural theory and risk: a review." Health, Risk & Society, 1(1), 71-90.

### Core Argument

Risk perception is not purely objective or individual - it is shaped by cultural and social factors. Different social structures produce different ways of understanding and responding to risk.

---

## The Grid/Group Model

The model uses two dimensions to categorize different worldviews and risk perceptions:

### GROUP Dimension (Horizontal Axis)

**Definition:** The extent to which individuals are bound by group norms and values.

| Low Group | High Group |
|-----------|------------|
| Emphasizes individualism | Emphasizes strong social bonds |
| Values autonomy | Values collective identity |
| Individual decision-making | Group-based decision-making |

### GRID Dimension (Vertical Axis)

**Definition:** The extent to which individuals are constrained by rules and social categorizations.

| High Grid | Low Grid |
|-----------|----------|
| Strong hierarchy | Fluid social environment |
| Clearly defined social roles | Less regulated interactions |
| Strict rules and categorizations | More flexibility in roles |

## The Four Risk Perception Types

**Important Warning:** These are stereotypes used for analytical purposes, not fixed personality types.

### 1. Hierarchists (High Group, High Grid)

**Position:** Upper right quadrant

**Characteristics:**
- Value strict social roles, clear rules, and strong social bonds
- Trust established authorities and experts to manage risks
- Believe in institutional management of problems

**View of Nature:** Nature is tolerant within limits (perverse)
- Requires careful management and regulation
- Can handle some stress but needs oversight

**Approach to Risk:** "Manage and regulate"

**Stereotypical Hero:** The stern bureaucrat of the positional hierarchy

**Response to Knowledge Withholding:**
- "People have a responsibility to find out the dangers they face"
- "Public protest is necessary to ensure good quality information"

---

### 2. Individualists (Low Group, Low Grid)

**Position:** Lower left quadrant

**Characteristics:**
- Emphasize personal autonomy, minimal rules, and individual freedoms
- See risks as opportunities for innovation
- Believe markets and personal responsibility are the best ways to manage risks

**View of Nature:** Nature is resilient
- Can bounce back from disruptions
- Self-correcting systems

**Approach to Risk:** "Go for it"

**Stereotypical Hero:** The proud pioneer of the free market

**Response to Knowledge Withholding:**
- "People should trust themselves to get informed on personal (inner) risks"
- "People should find out whom to trust regarding quality knowledge for societal (outer) risks"

---

### 3. Egalitarians (High Group, Low Grid)

**Position:** Lower right quadrant

**Characteristics:**
- Prioritize collective decision-making and shared resources
- Strong sense of community
- Distrust hierarchies and commercial interests

**View of Nature:** Nature is vulnerable
- Fragile and easily disrupted by human activity
- Requires protection

**Approach to Risk:** "Better safe than sorry" (Precautionary Principle)

**Stereotypical Hero:** The charismatic saint of the sectarian enclave

**Response to Knowledge Withholding:**
- "Governments and corporations cannot be trusted to inform because they are fuelled by vested interests"
- "Knowledge is corrupted even when it is made available"

---

### 4. Fatalists/Isolates (High Grid, Low Group)

**Position:** Upper left quadrant

**Characteristics:**
- Characterized by strict rules but limited social interaction
- Feel powerless in the face of external forces
- Believe outcomes are largely beyond their control
- Accept risks as inevitable

**View of Nature:** Nature is capricious (unpredictable)
- Random and uncontrollable
- No point trying to manage it

**Approach to Risk:** "Come what may"

**Stereotypical Hero:** Fatalists do not have heroes (according to Douglas, 2007)

**Response to Knowledge Withholding:**
- "Science cannot be trusted, so knowledge is never reliable"
- "We all opt out of society every day so we deserve to get what we avoid solving"

## Applying the Model: Practice Examples

### Example 1: AI Position (Hierarchist)

> "AI could be a powerful tool for societal efficiency. We should develop it within a framework of ethical guidelines and expert oversight. Important to maintain control and prevent disruption to established systems."

**Analysis:** This is a **Hierarchist** position because it:
- Trusts expert oversight
- Values ethical guidelines (rules)
- Wants to maintain established systems
- Sees technology as manageable through proper institutional frameworks

### Example 2: GMO Position (Fatalist)

> "GMOs are just another development that we have little control over. We might consume them without much thought. Our fate is largely predetermined regardless of what we eat."

**Analysis:** This is a **Fatalist** position because it:
- Expresses powerlessness
- Sees outcomes as predetermined
- Shows passive acceptance of risk
- Lacks agency in decision-making

## Relevance to RRI and Problem Wickedness

### Why Different Risk Perceptions Matter

1. **Contributes to wicked problems:** When stakeholders have fundamentally different risk perceptions, finding common ground becomes difficult

2. **Affects technology acceptance:** Different groups will accept or reject technologies based on their underlying worldview, not just "the facts"

3. **Shapes policy debates:** Understanding these different rationalities helps explain why debates persist despite scientific evidence

4. **Informs anticipation:** Different groups will anticipate different consequences from the same technology

### Using This Framework in Analysis

When analyzing actor statements in controversies:
1. Identify language that signals grid/group position
2. Look for views on nature, authority, and collective action
3. Consider how risk perception shapes their policy recommendations
4. Assess whether conflicting risk perceptions contribute to problem wickedness

## Exam Preparation: Key Takeaways

1. **Risk is culturally constructed** - not purely objective calculation of likelihood x severity

2. **Grid/Group model has two dimensions:**
   - Grid = rules and hierarchy
   - Group = social bonds and collective identity

3. **Four worldviews produce four risk rationalities:**
   - Hierarchist: Trust experts, manage carefully
   - Individualist: Trust markets, embrace opportunity
   - Egalitarian: Trust community, apply precaution
   - Fatalist: Trust nothing, accept fate

4. **Each worldview has a corresponding philosophy of nature:**
   - Tolerant within limits, Resilient, Vulnerable, Capricious

5. **Only ~14% of survey respondents** fit neatly into these stereotypes - most people are more complex

6. **Application:** Use this framework to analyze why different actors in technological debates talk past each other and why some problems become "wicked"
