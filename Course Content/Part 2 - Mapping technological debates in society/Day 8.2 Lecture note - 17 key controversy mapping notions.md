# 17 Key Controversy Mapping Notions

This document presents the 17 essential concepts for controversy mapping methodology in Science, Technology & Society (STS). These notions form the theoretical and practical foundation for analyzing technological debates.

## 1. Post-demographics (Rogers)

**Scholar:** Richard Rogers

**Definition:** A way to study personal data in social networking platforms, focusing on how profiling is performed and its consequences, departing from traditional demographic research methods.

**Key Insight:** We do not have to pretend that a user account matches a person (which is not true in general). We can still study user accounts sociologically.

**Application to Controversy Mapping:**
- Study statements of actors in the digital public space
- Do not pretend statements are "representative" of thoughts in a traditional sense
- Study statements for what they are: **traces**

**Exam Tip:** Post-demographics allows us to analyze digital content without claiming it represents what people "really think" - we analyze traces, not demographics.

**Further Reading:** [Richard Rogers on post-demographics](https://www.digitalmethods.net/Digitalmethods/PostDemographics)

## 2. Just Observe (Latour)

**Scholar:** Bruno Latour (developed pedagogically by Tommaso Venturini)

**Definition:** A methodological stance emphasizing careful, detailed observation of controversies as they unfold, without imposing predetermined theoretical frameworks or rushing to resolve disputes.

**Core Quote:** *"Just observe and describe controversies"* - Bruno Latour

**Why It's Hard (Venturini):** The difficulty lies in refraining from:
- Writing analysis using your favorite theories
- Imposing your personal perspective on the subject
- Relying on your own certainties

**Venturini's Three Practical Guidelines:**
1. Do not restrain observation to any single theory or methodology
2. Observe from as many viewpoints as possible
3. Listen to actors' voices more than to your own presumptions

**Application:** Approach controversies with openness rather than predetermined conclusions.

**Further Reading:** [Venturini's "Diving in Magma" (2011)](https://www.tommasoventurini.it/wp/wp-content/uploads/2011/08/DivingInMagma.pdf)

## 3. Second-Degree Objectivity (Latour)

**Scholar:** Bruno Latour / Tommaso Venturini

**Definition:** Being objective about what actors say and do regarding disputed facts, rather than trying to objectively determine which facts are true.

**The Problem:** Within a controversy, the absence of agreement between actors prevents us from finding "the objective truth."

**The Solution:**
- **First-degree objectivity** = Complete neutrality (impossible in controversies)
- **Second-degree objectivity** = Rigorous analysis of actors' positions

**Application to Controversy Mapping:** Document and analyze the claims actors make without taking sides on whose claims are factually correct.

**Exam Tip:** Second-degree objectivity shifts the object of study from "what is true" to "what actors claim is true."

**Further Reading:** [Venturini's "Diving in Magma" (2011)](https://www.tommasoventurini.it/wp/wp-content/uploads/2011/08/DivingInMagma.pdf)

## 4. Exploratory Data Analysis (EDA)

**Definition:** A data analysis approach focused on finding questions rather than confirming hypotheses.

**Two Key Distinctions:**

| Comparison | Purpose | Focus |
|------------|---------|-------|
| **Exploratory vs. Confirmatory** | Find questions vs. Find answers | Discovery vs. Hypothesis testing |
| **Exploratory vs. Explanatory** | Visualize for yourself vs. Visualize for others | Build internal knowledge vs. Communicate efficiently |

**Application to Controversy Mapping:**
- **Exploration phase:** Pivot quickly between data facets, build internal knowledge
- **Explanation phase:** Communicate findings efficiently (see "visual storytelling")

**Exam Tip:** Same tools can be used for both, but the strategies differ fundamentally.

**Further Reading:** [Guide to Exploratory Data Analysis](https://medium.com/data-science/a-data-scientists-essential-guide-to-exploratory-data-analysis-25637eee0cf6)

## 5. Close / Distant Reading (Moretti)

**Scholar:** Franco Moretti

**Definition:** Two complementary approaches to analyzing texts and data.

| Type | Description | Transformation |
|------|-------------|----------------|
| **Close reading** | Traditional reading | Source material is NOT transformed |
| **Distant reading** | Algorithmic/visual analysis | Source material IS transformed to make patterns appear |

**Application to Controversy Mapping:** Exploring digital public discourse about a project (e.g., Energy Islands) through algorithms and visualization qualifies as **distant reading**.

**Exam Tip:** Distant reading reveals patterns that would be invisible in close reading of individual documents.

**Further Reading:** [Introduction to Distant Reading by Walsh & Horowitz](https://walshbr.com/textanalysiscoursebook/book/reading-at-scale/distant-reading/)

## 6. Quali-Quantitative Analysis

**Definition:** An analytical approach that combines qualitative and quantitative methods by alternating between them, rather than applying both simultaneously.

**Comparison of Pure Approaches:**

| Qualitative | Quantitative |
|-------------|--------------|
| Interpretations and contexts | Measurements and statistical patterns |
| Interviews, observations, long texts | Surveys, datasets, experimental data |
| Richness and complexity | General trends and correlations |
| Close reading | Distant reading |

**What Quali-Quantitative Analysis Is:**
- Preserves richness of qualitative insights
- Leverages scalability and structure of quantitative data
- Aims to provide **thick description at scale**
- **Alternates** between qualitative moments and quantitative moments

**What It Is NOT:** Doing quali and quanti at the same time.

**Application:** In digital sociology, analyze large datasets while maintaining attention to meaning, context, and narrative.

**Further Reading:** [Venturini's "Diving in Magma" (2011)](https://www.tommasoventurini.it/wp/wp-content/uploads/2011/08/DivingInMagma.pdf)

## 7. Principle of Symmetry (Bloor)

**Scholar:** David Bloor

**Definition:** Part of Bloor's "strong programme" for the sociology of scientific knowledge. It demands that the same types of explanations are used for successful and unsuccessful knowledge claims alike.

**Core Principle:** Our methods should be able to analyze "bad science" as well as "good science."

**Example:** We should be able to understand the knowledge claims of flat earthers as well as the knowledge claims of renowned physicists.

**Important Clarification:** Treating claims symmetrically does NOT mean considering them equal in validity (see "Weighing positions" below).

**Application to Controversy Mapping:** Apply the same analytical rigor to all actors' claims, regardless of whether you agree with them.

**Further Reading:** [Wikipedia: Strong programme](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strong_programme)

## 8. Actor (in Actor-Network Theory)

**Scholar:** Bruno Latour (ANT)

**Definition:** In Actor-Network Theory (ANT), an actor is someone or something that **makes a difference** to the situation.

**Key Characteristics:**
- Not every person is an actor (people who do not make a difference are not actors)
- **Non-human actors** can include:
  - Organizations (agencies, companies, associations)
  - Animals, rivers, technologies
- Non-humans qualify as actors insofar as they make a difference

**Application to Controversy Mapping:** An actor is relevant because it:
- Makes knowledge claims (involving expertise or risk)
- Influences decisions
- Answers open questions
- Shapes public debate
- Mobilizes new actors
- Makes a difference in any other way

**Exam Tip:** The defining criterion is whether the entity *makes a difference*, not whether it is human.

**Further Reading:** [Latour's "Where Are the Missing Masses?" (1992)](http://www.bruno-latour.fr/sites/default/files/50-MISSING-MASSES-GB.pdf)

## 9. Follow the Actors (Latour)

**Scholar:** Bruno Latour

**Definition:** A methodological principle of investigating according to actors' beliefs and knowledge rather than our own, listening to their knowledge claims and arguments to retrace their underlying logic.

**Why This Matters:** Investigating according to our own beliefs would prevent us from accounting for the knowledge claims of actors we disagree with.

**Practical Implementation:**
- Pay special attention to specific terms and arguments used by different actors
- Do NOT replace their words with yours (even when you think they are the same)
- Follow actors to uncomfortable places you disagree with
- Account for what you find

**Critical for:** Using queries to investigate a dataset - use actors' terminology, not your own.

**Connection:** This principle follows from the **principle of symmetry**.

**Further Reading:** [Marres & Moats' "Mapping Controversies with Social Media" (2015)](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=2567929)

## 10. Weighing Positions

**Definition:** The analytical task of showing that some actors are stronger and make a bigger difference than others.

**Relationship to Symmetry:**
- **Principle of symmetry** = Treat all actors equally (describe fairly regardless of agreement)
- **Weighing positions** = Show that actors are NOT equal in influence

**These are compatible:** Fair treatment does not mean pretending actors have equal impact.

**Ways Actors Differ in Weight:**
- Their knowledge claims were repeated by more other actors
- They convinced more policy makers to take action
- They were more influential on what topics are debated on social media

**Application:** Following second-degree objectivity, provide an **objective measurement** of each actor's ability to make a difference.

**Exam Tip:** Symmetry is about method; weighing is about results. You can analyze all actors fairly while still showing some have more influence.

## 11. Measurements

**Definition:** In sociology, measurements refer to the practical work of making social life knowable, comparable, and actionable within different research communities (or networks of practice).

**What Constitutes Measurements:** Visualizations and numbers obtained from datasets through analytical notebooks.

**Post-demographic Perspective on Measurements:**
- Our corpus does NOT represent "what people say" about a topic
- We neither measure nor aim to measure "what people say"
- **However:** Our measurements can be compared, contrasted, and contextualized with one another
- This generates valid insights even without traditional representativeness

**Exam Tip:** Measurements are valid for comparison and contextualization, not for claiming to represent public opinion.

## 12. Circulating Reference (Latour)

**Scholar:** Bruno Latour

**Definition:** Scientific facts gain their power to represent the world by moving through chains of transformations where each step changes the format but maintains the connection. Facts don't just mirror reality directly.

**Example Chain of Reference (Energy Islands Project):**

1. What people say online → Series of web documents to harvest
2. Web documents → Structured table of actor statements (with metadata)
3. Raw statements → Homogeneous, normalized statements (translation)
4. Normalized statements → Enriched data points (semantic map, clusters, issues coding)
5. Dataset → Measurements and visualizations (using notebooks)
6. Visualizations/measurements → Arguments in policy brief

**Critical Point:** Your argument is well-grounded insofar as it maintains an **unbroken chain of transformation** that actually refers to the original data. A single broken link (e.g., misusing a visualization) can make your entire argument fall apart.

**Further Reading:** [Latour's "Circulating Reference" in Pandora's Hope (1999)](http://www.bruno-latour.fr/sites/default/files/downloads/53-PANDORA-TOPOFIL-pdf.pdf)

## 13. Truth and Robustness

**Scholar:** Bruno Latour

**Definition:** Arguments do not become robust because they are inherently true; rather, they **start becoming true as they get more robust**.

**Key Insight:** We must invest considerable effort in consolidating our arguments. It is not enough to be convinced of our analysis's validity; we must demonstrate to others what makes our reasoning resilient to criticism and counterarguments.

**In Controversy Analysis:**
- Competing versions of "truth" emerge from disagreements among actors
- We must **deconstruct** others' arguments (examine foundations, assess robustness)
- We must also **construct** compelling arguments of our own

**Takeaway:** Solid arguments have a **specific structure** that lets the audience:
- Easily follow your reasoning
- See what evidence you're using
- Understand how you build your case from that evidence

**Further Reading:** [Latour's "Opening Pandora's Black Box" in Science in Action (1987)](https://thehangedman.com/teaching-files/svd-phd/1-latour/latour-SIA-0-introduction.pdf)

## 14. The Toulmin Model for Building Arguments

**Scholar:** Stephen Toulmin (1958)

**Definition:** A model for understanding how arguments are structured and categorized, consisting of six parts.

**The Six Components:**

| Component | Definition | Role |
|-----------|------------|------|
| **Claim** | A statement that something is so | What you're arguing |
| **Grounds** | The backing for the claim | Data and measurements |
| **Warrant** | Bridge-like statement explaining why the claim follows from the grounds | General rule justifying the claim |
| **Backing** | Support or explanation provided for the warrant | Field-dependent support |
| **Qualifier** | The degree of certainty offered for the claim | The modality |
| **Rebuttal** | Exceptions to the claim | Limitations acknowledged |

**Key Takeaway:** A robust argument is NOT just a claim backed by data (grounds). The crucial elements are:
- **Warrant:** Why does the claim follow from the grounds?
- **Backing:** What supports the warrant?

**Application:** To make better arguments, learn to make your **warrant and backing explicit**.

**Further Reading:** [Purdue OWL: Toulmin Argument](https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/general_writing/academic_writing/historical_perspectives_on_argumentation/toulmin_argument.html)

## 15. Technical Mediation

**Scholar:** Bruno Latour (STS concept)

**Definition:** Technologies are mediators rather than mere intermediaries - they translate, modify, and redirect the interactions they facilitate rather than transmitting information neutrally.

**Core Principle:** **Technical mediation is non-neutral.**

**Implication for Analysis:**
- As we use tools (algorithms) to process data and make measurements, mediations pile up
- Each step is another link in the chain of circulating reference
- Each mediation's non-neutrality must be accounted for
- When producing a visualization, we must explain what is happening at each mediation

**Exam Tip:** Every tool, algorithm, or transformation introduces bias that must be acknowledged and explained.

**Further Reading:** [Latour's "On technical mediation" (1994)](http://www.bruno-latour.fr/sites/default/files/54-TECHNIQUES-GB.pdf)

## 16. Explanatory Visualization for Visual Arguments

**Definition:** The practice of creating visualizations that communicate context to an audience, distinct from exploratory visualization.

**Key Distinction:**
- **Exploration:** Visualize for yourself (build internal knowledge, pivot quickly)
- **Explanation:** Visualize for others (communicate context)

**Classic Advice:**
1. Prioritize **clarity** above everything else
2. Minimize **distortions** (visualization is a mediation, so not neutral)

**Building Visual Arguments (Connecting to Toulmin):**
- Highlight the **visual pattern** you want the reader to see
- Explicitly state **why your reading follows** from the visual pattern
- This provides the context readers need to follow your argument

**Application:** In a policy brief, unpack the mediation for the reader - connect visual evidence to claims with explicit warrants.

## 17. Annotation (of Data Visualizations)

**Definition:** The practice of adding textual labels, markers, and explanations directly on visualizations to guide readers' attention and interpretation.

**Why Annotation is Necessary:**
- After exploration, your brain is loaded with context
- When communicating, you must **externalize this internalized knowledge**
- Your audience needs access to the context you've built

**Elements of Visual Communication:**
- Title
- Legend
- Textual explanations
- **Annotations** (your best tool)

**What Annotations Do:**
1. Guide reader's eyes to the **visual pattern** you need them to see
2. State **what the pattern means**
3. Explain **how your interpretation follows** from the pattern (like a warrant)
4. Provide backing for your interpretation

**Best Practice:** Annotations should be **in-place**, i.e., close to the pattern itself.

**Further Reading:** [10 Rules for Graph Annotations](https://speakingppt.com/10-rules-for-graph-annotations/)

## Concept Connections

Understanding how these notions relate to each other is essential for exam preparation:

**Methodological Foundations:**
- **Just observe** + **Second-degree objectivity** + **Principle of symmetry** + **Follow the actors** = The core methodological stance

**Data Analysis Approach:**
- **Post-demographics** → How we treat digital data
- **EDA** + **Close/distant reading** + **Quali-quantitative analysis** → How we analyze data
- **Measurements** → What we produce from data

**Maintaining Rigor:**
- **Circulating reference** → Keeping the chain intact
- **Technical mediation** → Acknowledging non-neutrality at each step

**Building Arguments:**
- **Weighing positions** → Showing differential actor influence
- **Truth and robustness** → Why structure matters
- **Toulmin model** → How to structure arguments
- **Explanatory visualization** + **Annotation** → How to communicate visually
