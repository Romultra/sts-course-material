## 1. Core Methodological Concept: "Follow the Actors"
This approach is the practical application of the **Principle of Symmetry**. It requires researchers to suspend their own beliefs and trace the associations made by the actors themselves.

### The "Actor" in Actor-Network Theory (ANT)
* **Definition:** An actor is defined not by *who* they are, but by *what* they do. An actor is "someone or something that makes a difference to the situation".
* **Non-Humans:** Humans, organizations, animals, rivers, and technologies can all be actors if they influence the controversy.
* [cite_start]**Scope:** Researchers track actors only insofar as they make knowledge claims, influence decisions, or shape public debate [cite: 1073-1074].

### How to Follow Them
1.  **Listen:** Use close reading to understand their arguments.
2.  [cite_start]**Adopt their language:** Do not replace their specific terms with your own academic jargon[cite: 1083].
3.  [cite_start]**Use their words as tools:** Leverage their native terms as **markers** (keywords) for query-based methods[cite: 1084].
4.  [cite_start]**Go to uncomfortable places:** Follow the logic of actors you fundamentally disagree with (e.g., Flat Earthers) to understand their world-building[cite: 1069, 1079].

## 2. Theoretical Pillars

### A. The Principle of Symmetry
[cite_start]Rooted in **David Bloor's** "Strong Programme" for the sociology of scientific knowledge [cite: 1058-1059]. It is essential for maintaining **Second-Degree Objectivity**.

* [cite_start]**The Rule:** The same types of explanations must be used for *successful* (true/accepted) and *unsuccessful* (false/rejected) knowledge claims[cite: 1064].
* **Implication:** You cannot explain a "correct" scientific theory by saying "because it is true" while explaining a "false" theory by saying "because of social bias." [cite_start]Both must be analyzed through the conditions (social, psychological, cultural) that brought them about[cite: 1062].
* [cite_start]**Application:** We must understand the claims of "flat earthers" with the same sociological tools we use for renowned physicists[cite: 1069].

### B. Weighing Positions (Symmetry $\neq$ Equality)
A common misconception is that "symmetry" means pretending all actors are equally important.
* **The Distinction:** We *treat* all actors fairly (symmetry), but we do not pretend they are *equal* in impact.
* **The Mandate:** You must **weigh** the actors. [cite_start]Your analysis must quantitatively show that some actors make a bigger difference than others [cite: 1086-1087].
* **Metrics for Weighing:**
    * Repetition of their claims by others.
    * Influence on policymakers.
    * [cite_start]Dominance in social media debates [cite: 1090-1093].

## 3. Analytical Frameworks: Reading the Data

### Close vs. Distant Reading (Franco Moretti)
Mapping involves navigating between two modes of reading:
* **Close Reading:** Traditional interpretive reading of the source text. It focuses on context and richness.
* **Distant Reading:** Transforming source material into abstract patterns (e.g., graphs, maps) to see overviews. [cite_start]Moretti calls it "a little pact with the devil: we know how to read texts, now let's learn how *not* to read them"[cite: 1000].
    * [cite_start]*Note:* The Energy Islands Issue Atlas is a form of **distant reading**[cite: 1003].

### Quali-Quantitative Analysis
[cite_start]This method does not do qualitative and quantitative analysis *simultaneously*, but **alternates** between them[cite: 1029].
* [cite_start]**Qualitative Moments:** Focus on interpretation, context, and complexity (Close Reading) [cite: 1019-1021].
* [cite_start]**Quantitative Moments:** Focus on measurement, scale, and statistical patterns (Distant Reading) [cite: 1022-1024].

## 4. Practical Tool: Query-Based Methods
[cite_start]Queries act as a "fishing net" to capture specific slices of the controversy from the massive dataset[cite: 1031].

### Designing Good Queries
[cite_start]Query design is about the **selective effect** of keywords[cite: 1056].

| **Keyword Type** | **Status** | **Examples** | **Reason** |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Proper Nouns** | **Good** | "AstraZeneca", "Anthony Fauci" | [cite_start]Highly specific and selective[cite: 1040]. |
| **Expert Terms** | **Good** | "B 1.1.7", "Hydroxychloroquine" | [cite_start]Identifies technical discourse[cite: 1042]. |
| **Specific Slang** | **Good** | "hodl", "OK boomer" | [cite_start]Captures specific sub-groups/cultures[cite: 1044]. |
| **Ambiguous Terms**| **Bad** | Common names, Polysemic terms | [cite_start]Catches too much irrelevant "fish" [cite: 1047-1049]. |

[cite_start]**Tip:** Even "bad" resources (like conspiracy forums) provide "good" keywords because they reveal the alternative vocabulary used by specific publics (e.g., "China flu" vs. "Pandemic") [cite: 1051-1054].

## 5. Exam Application: The Investigation Workflow
For your Policy Brief, you are encouraged to structure your inquiry by alternating between levels.

### Strategy A: Qualitative $\rightarrow$ Quantitative
1.  **Read:** Select specific statements (e.g., regarding "Risk").
2.  **Contrast:** How do different actors frame "Risk"? (e.g., Statement 143 fears industrialization; Statement 5742 (Ørsted) fears climate change more than bird death).
3.  **Hypothesize:** Formulate a theory about these differences.
4.  **Test:** Use a notebook query to measure if this pattern holds across the whole dataset.

### Strategy B: Quantitative $\rightarrow$ Qualitative
1.  **Visualize:** Use a notebook to find a distribution (e.g., "Issue X peaks in 2023").
2.  **Hypothesize:** Interpret the visualization.
3.  **Validate:** Go back to the text. Find 1-5 specific actor statements that explain *why* that peak happened.
