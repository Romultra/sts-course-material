# Day 9.1: Measuring Social Acceptance - The Case of Carbon Capture and Storage (CCS)

**Guest Lecture by Professor Jacob Ladenburg, DTU Management**

---

## Overview

This lecture examines how to measure and understand **social acceptance** of green technologies, using Carbon Capture and Storage (CCS) as a case study. The insights are directly applicable to analyzing technology controversies and developing policy recommendations.

---

## What is Social Acceptance?

**Social acceptance** refers to the willingness of individuals, communities, and stakeholders to support and integrate new technologies into society (Gaede and Rowlands, 2018).

Social acceptance encompasses:
- **Public attitudes, perceptions, and beliefs** towards technology projects
- Integration of **technological, economic, environmental, and ethical** considerations
- Both **general population** views and **site-specific** local concerns

> Understanding social acceptance is crucial for building a sustainable and inclusive transition to a low-carbon future.

---

## Why Does Social Acceptance Matter?

| Importance | Description |
|------------|-------------|
| **Successful Implementation** | Social acceptance fosters collaboration, support, and buy-in from various stakeholders |
| **Reduced Resistance** | Overcoming opposition and skepticism through informed public engagement |
| **Policy Relevance** | Helps policymakers design effective communication and engagement strategies |

---

## Theoretical Model of Acceptance

Based on **Huijts et al. (2012)** and **Zuch and Ladenburg (2023)**, acceptance is shaped by multiple interacting factors:

### Information Inputs
- **Baseline Information** - General introduction to the technology
- **Experimental Information** - Additional targeted information (tested through RCTs)

### Experience and Knowledge
- **Experience**: Direct encounters with projects or related technologies impact perceptions
- **Knowledge**: Understanding of processes, benefits, and risks influences acceptance
  - Generally **low level of knowledge** among public about CCS
  - Baseline information is often provided to establish common understanding

### Psychological Factors (Individual Level)

| Factor | Description |
|--------|-------------|
| **Trust** | Confidence in regulatory bodies, industry, and technology developers |
| **Fairness** | Perceived equitable distribution of benefits and burdens |
| **Affect** | Emotional reactions and positive/negative associations |
| **Perceived Costs, Benefits, and Risks** | Assessment of economic, environmental, and social impacts |
| **Efficacy** | Belief in the effectiveness of the technology |
| **Social Norms** | Alignment with societal values |
| **Personal Norms** | Alignment with personal beliefs |
| **Attitude** | Overall favorable/unfavorable opinions |
| **Perceived Behavioral Control** | Belief in one's ability to influence or participate in decisions |

These factors flow through **Intention to Accept** and ultimately determine **Acceptance**.

---

## Drivers of Social Acceptance

1. **Environmental Concerns** - Acknowledging the urgent need for climate change mitigation and reduced greenhouse gas emissions (Itaoka et al., 2009)

2. **Economic Opportunities** - Recognizing the potential for job creation, industry growth, and economic development (von Rothkirch and Ejderyan, 2021)

3. **Technological Innovation** - Embracing CCS as a bridge to a cleaner energy future while renewable technologies advance (Braun et al., 2018)

---

## Challenges to Social Acceptance

1. **Risk Perception** - Addressing fears related to potential accidents, leaks, and long-term consequences (Karimi & Toikka, 2014)

2. **Equity and Justice** - Ensuring fair distribution of benefits and addressing potential negative impacts on vulnerable communities (Karytsas et al., 2023)

3. **Policy and Regulation** - Developing comprehensive frameworks to ensure safety, accountability, and effective governance

---

## Levels of Social Acceptance

### Site or Project Specific
- Tailored questions addressing unique **local community concerns** about a specific existing or proposed project (Oltra et al., 2022)
- Examples: Acceptance of local gas storage facility; acceptance of gas transport in local area

### General Population
- Broader inquiries into wider **societal perceptions and trends** (Ladenburg et al., 2023)
- Acceptance of technology use in a broader geographical unit (municipality, region, country, EU) without referring to a specific project

---

## Methods for Measuring Social Acceptance

### Data Sources

**Surveys** (Dillman et al., 2014; Karytsas et al., 2023; Ladenburg et al., 2023)
- Structured questionnaires measuring public attitudes, concerns, and knowledge
- Can target: Local residents, national populations, survey company respondents, central register respondents

**Personal Interviews or Focus Groups** (Ha-Duong et al., 2009; Shackley et al., 2004)
- In-depth conversations allowing nuanced exploration of beliefs and opinions
- Using open questions or semi-structured questionnaires

### Data Analysis Methods
- **Ordinary least square/ordered logit regression** (Greene, 2012)
- **Mean comparisons** between groups
- **Transcription and coding** of interviews (Miles et al., 2019; Patton, 2014)
- **Topic coding** for qualitative data

## Investigating Heterogeneity in Acceptance

Analyzing acceptance variation across **socio-economic groups** provides valuable insights.

### Key Factors Influencing Perceptions
- Gender
- Age
- Education level
- Income
- Area of residence (urban vs. rural)

### Example Investigative Questions
- "How does gender impact the perception of CCS benefits and risks?"
- "Do different age groups have varying levels of support for CCS initiatives?"
- "Is there a correlation between education level and understanding of CCS technology?"
- "Does income level influence willingness to invest in CCS projects?"
- "How does geographic location affect attitudes towards CCS in urban and rural areas?"

## Case Study: Danish CCS Acceptance Research

### Research Question
> "How and to what extent does information provision affect public responses to CCS?"

### Survey Design

**Sample Characteristics:**
- Large-scale online survey on national energy perception
- Sent August 2022 to random sample of 55,000 Danish households via 'e-boks'
- Total sample: 12,251 respondents (22.3% response rate)
- Acceptance sub-section: 3,969 effective respondents

### Experimental Design

**Information Experiment (3 conditions):**

| Condition | Content |
|-----------|---------|
| **INFO_BI** (Baseline) | Basic explanation of CCS technology and Danish North Sea storage testing |
| **INFO_A** (Experience Abroad) | Baseline + "The technology has been used abroad for almost 40 years" |
| **INFO_DK** (Experience Denmark) | Baseline + Information about Danish gas storage experience since 1980s-90s |

### Acceptance Question
> "To what extent should Denmark use the following measures to reduce CO2 emissions?"

Response scale: Very small extent - Small extent - Moderate extent - Large extent - Very large extent

**CCS Location Options:**
- Offshore
- Nearshore
- Onshore Rural
- Onshore Urban

### Willingness to Pay (WTP) Study

**Valuation Scenarios:**
- 5 MT CCS: Capture and store 5 million tons of CO2 per year (~23% of reduction needs)
- 10 MT CCS: Capture and store 10 million tons of CO2 per year (~47% of reduction needs)

**Method:** Contingent Valuation Method (CVM) with double-bounded dichotomous choice

---

## Key Findings

### Information Effects on CCS Acceptance

| Variable | Offshore | Nearshore | Onshore Rural | Onshore Urban |
|----------|----------|-----------|---------------|---------------|
| INFO_A (abroad) | 0.057 | **0.117*** | **0.101*** | 0.068 |
| INFO_DK (Denmark) | 0.029 | 0.078 | **0.131**** | **0.010*** |
| CCS_FAM (prior familiarity) | **0.097*** | **0.118**** | **0.113**** | 0.057 |

*Significance: * p < 0.05, ** p < 0.01, *** p < 0.001*

### Key Insights

**1. Information Provision Matters**
- Information about international CCS experience (INFO_A) increases acceptance of **nearshore and rural onshore** CCS
- Information about Danish gas storage experience (INFO_DK) increases acceptance of **onshore CCS** (rural & urban)
- **No significant impact** on offshore CCS acceptance

**2. Prior Familiarity Increases Acceptance**
- Prior CCS familiarity (CCS_FAM) positively affects acceptance across most locations
- Also increases willingness to pay for CCS

**3. Question Order Effects**
- Strong priming effects: receiving onshore CCS options first **decreases** acceptance of nearshore and offshore CCS
- But **increases** onshore urban CCS acceptance

**4. Context Sensitivity**
- Information effects depend on:
  - The information content provided
  - The proposed location of CCS
  - The project's scope (emission reduction potential)

### WTP Results

| Variable | 5 MT CCS | 10 MT CCS |
|----------|----------|-----------|
| CCS_FAM | **62.44 DKK**** | **76.07 DKK*** |
| INFO_A | 27.07 | 36.47 |
| INFO_DK | **49.68 DKK*** | -40.77 |

- Prior familiarity increases WTP for both scenarios
- Danish experience information increases WTP only for smaller-scale (5 MT) CCS
- Effect of information on WTP depends on project scale

---

## Conclusions

1. **Social acceptance of CCS is multifaceted**, involving experience, knowledge, and intricate psychological factors

2. **Information provision can increase acceptance**, but effects are highly context-sensitive

3. **Prior familiarity** with technology consistently increases both acceptance and willingness to pay

4. **Location matters** - different information types affect acceptance differently depending on where the technology is proposed

5. **Preferences may be more sensitive** to external informational cues than general acceptance statements

6. **Tailored communication strategies** are needed for different socio-economic groups and geographical contexts

---

## Implications for Responsible Research and Innovation (RRI)

Understanding social acceptance is essential for:

- **Early stakeholder engagement** - Involving communities before decisions are made
- **Transparent communication** - Providing balanced information about risks and benefits
- **Addressing fairness concerns** - Ensuring equitable distribution of impacts
- **Building trust** - Through consistent, honest dialogue with affected communities
- **Adaptive governance** - Adjusting approaches based on public feedback
