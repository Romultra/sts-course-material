## Key notions

### Technical mediation
In Science and Technology Studies (STS), mediation is a core concept that challenges the idea that technology simply transmits or carries information neutrally from one point to another. **In short: technical mediation is non-neutral.**

### Annotations
After an exploration phase, your brain is loaded with context about the data. But when you transition to communicating your results to an audience, you need to externalize this internalized knowledge so that your audience has access to it. Your visualizations must feature a title, a legend, textual explanations, all of which can communicate the context. But your best tool is to *annotate* the visualization.



Annotation is a way to guide your reader's eyes to the visual pattern you need them to see. The textual content of the annotation allows you to state what the pattern means. Your text explanations should also state with clarity how your interpretation follows from the visual pattern (like a warrant does in the Toulmin system) which may require some backing (again, like in the Toulmin system).

As an annotation points to a key visual pattern, it is always better *in-place*, i.e. close to the pattern itself.



Read more: 10 good practical tips about annotating data visualizations: [https://speakingppt.com/10-rules-for-graph-annotations/](https://speakingppt.com/10-rules-for-graph-annotations/)

## Activity instructions

This is also like a mini writing workshop, but "writing" in the sense of graphic design (which is a form of writing).

Start from the argument you have built during the last activity. You could use or make another one if you prefer, but it must be built on a query-based measurement of the actor statement corpus.

First, individually and independently, each of you produces a complete visualization communicating the chosen argument. A complete visualization must include:

* a clean visual rendering of the measurement;
* a legend (in-place if possible) providing the information necessary for a non-expert audience to parse the visualization;
* in-place annotations highlighting the visual pattern(s) supporting the measurement;
* (optionally) a statement of what the pattern is:
* a statement of what the pattern means (i.e., the grounds of your argument);
* a short text making and defending the claim, on the ground of the measurement visualized;
* the short text must also provide any context necessary to understand the argument and its relevance for the Energy Islands project.

This step should take no longer than 20 minutes.

Then in the remaining time, share, compare and criticize the results within the group and compose the best possible visualization using the best ideas from everyone.

Pay attention to how other members of your group have had quite different strategies to tackle the same question, that some strategies produce better results than others, and that it is not always clear which one is better and why until you have tried it. You may come to the conclusion that it is worth exploring multiple approaches to operationalizing a same question.

*Note: if you feel like the task is to difficult to do individually, just do it all as a group (but this will leave less room for good solutions to emerge).*
