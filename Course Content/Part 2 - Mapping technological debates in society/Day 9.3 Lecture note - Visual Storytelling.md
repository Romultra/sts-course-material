# Day 9.3: Visual Storytelling

> **Core message:** Explaining visually is a form of writing. Visualization as technical mediation is non-neutral.

---

## Learning Objectives

After this session, you should be able to:
- Distinguish between **exploratory** and **explanatory** visualization
- Recognize that visualization involves **technical mediation** that is never neutral
- Apply the **five-step process** for visual argumentation
- Understand why and how to **annotate** visualizations effectively

---

## 1. Explanatory vs. Exploratory Visualization

| Aspect | Exploratory Visualization | Explanatory Visualization |
|--------|---------------------------|---------------------------|
| **Purpose** | Discover patterns, generate insights | Communicate a specific message |
| **Audience** | The analyst/researcher themselves | External audience |
| **Focus** | Open-ended exploration | Efficient message delivery |
| **Process stage** | Early analysis phase | Final communication phase |

**Explanatory visualization** aims to convey a message as efficiently as possible. It is designed for an audience and focuses on clarity of communication.

### Key Insight
You should **first visualize for yourself** (exploratory), **then worry about the audience** (explanatory). The transition from exploration to explanation is crucial for effective visual communication.

---

## 2. Visualization as Technical Mediation

### The Non-Neutrality Principle

**Technical mediation** refers to how technology shapes and transforms the information it presents. Visualization is never a neutral window onto data - it always involves choices that affect interpretation.

> When we produce a visualization, we need to be able to explain what is going on for each mediation involved.

### Why Visualizations Can Mislead

Design choices in visualization constitute forms of mediation that shape meaning:
- **Axis scaling** (starting at zero vs. arbitrary values)
- **Color choices** and emphasis
- **Data selection** and what is included/excluded
- **Chart type** selection

**Example:** The same Planned Parenthood data can appear dramatically different depending on whether the y-axis starts at zero or at an arbitrary value - one version is misleading, the other more accurate.

### Recommended Reading
- Edward Tufte: *The Visual Display of Quantitative Information*
- Alberto Cairo: *How Charts Lie*
- Hans Rosling: *Factfulness*

## 3. Visualization as a Form of Writing

Data visualization follows many of the same principles as good writing:

| Writing Principle | Applied to Visualization |
|-------------------|--------------------------|
| Never use complex words when simple ones will do | Never use a complex visualization when a simple one will do |
| First write for yourself, then revise for readers | First visualize for yourself, then worry about the audience |
| Be willing to cut and edit | Be like a butcher (you can always cut) |
| Every writer needs a reader | Every visualization needs an audience |
| Tension creates compelling narrative | Truth is a paradox - opposing ideas form the tension of a great story |
| Rules can be broken if done well | You can do whatever you want as long as you make it work |
| Show, don't tell | Show, don't tell (let the visual do the work) |

---

## 4. The Five-Step Visual Argumentation Process

When using visualization to make an argument, follow these core steps:

### Step 1: Produce the Visualization
- Generate the data visualization itself
- Use appropriate tools (e.g., notebooks, visualization software)
- Ensure data accuracy and appropriate chart selection

### Step 2: Provide the Necessary Context
- Add a clear, descriptive **title**
- Label and clarify **axes**
- Include **legends** where needed
- Specify **time periods**, scales, and units

### Step 3: Point to the Relevant Visual Pattern
- **Highlight** the specific pattern that supports your argument
- Use visual markers (circles, arrows, color emphasis)
- Draw the viewer's attention to what matters
- You may additionally state what the pattern is

### Step 4: Interpret What the Pattern Means
- Provide an **interpretation** of the highlighted pattern
- Connect the visual evidence to your argument
- Explain the significance of what is shown

### Step 5: Justify and Provide Larger Context
- Explain with more details in **accompanying text**
- Connect to broader themes and implications
- Provide necessary background information

## 5. Annotation Best Practices

**Annotations** are the key technique for externalizing context for your audience. They bridge the gap between what you know and what the viewer needs to understand.

### The In-Place Principle

> **Annotations should be in-place:** close to what they refer to.

This means placing explanatory text and markers directly adjacent to the visual elements they describe, rather than in a separate legend or footnote.

### Effective Annotation Includes:

| Element | Purpose | Example |
|---------|---------|---------|
| **Labels** | Identify data series | "Documents mentioning 'evaluation'" |
| **Context markers** | Clarify what elements represent | "All documents in dataset (different scale)" |
| **Time indicators** | Orient the viewer temporally | "TIME -->" arrow |
| **Interpretation boxes** | State what patterns mean | "From 1985 to 2015, X is more present than Y" |
| **Highlight shapes** | Draw attention to key areas | Circles, ovals around significant patterns |

### Tools for Annotation
Simple drawing tools are sufficient for adding annotations:
- Arrows pointing to specific data points
- Circles or ovals to highlight regions
- Text boxes with interpretive comments
- Color coding for emphasis

## Key Concepts Summary

| Concept | Definition |
|---------|------------|
| **Explanatory visualization** | Visualization designed to convey a specific message efficiently to an audience |
| **Exploratory visualization** | Visualization used to discover patterns and generate insights during analysis |
| **Technical mediation** | How technology shapes and transforms information (never neutral) |
| **Annotation** | Labels, markers, and text added to externalize context for the audience |
| **In-place annotation** | Placing explanatory elements close to what they describe |
| **Visual pattern** | A recognizable trend, relationship, or feature in the visualization |

## Exam Preparation

### Potential Exam Questions

1. **Explain the difference between exploratory and explanatory visualization. When would you use each?**

2. **What does it mean that visualization is a form of "technical mediation"? Why is this mediation non-neutral?**

3. **Describe the five-step process for using visualization as argumentation.**

4. **What is the "in-place" principle for annotations, and why is it important?**

5. **How does the principle "show, don't tell" apply to data visualization?**

### Key Takeaways for Exam

- Visualization is **never neutral** - design choices constitute technical mediation
- The process moves from **exploratory** (for yourself) to **explanatory** (for audience)
- **Annotations externalize context** that the creator knows but the audience does not
- Visual argumentation requires not just showing data, but **highlighting patterns**, **interpreting meaning**, and **providing context**
- The same data can be visualized in misleading or accurate ways depending on choices made

## Connection to Controversy Mapping

In controversy mapping, visual storytelling is essential for:
- Presenting network visualizations of actor relationships
- Showing temporal evolution of debates
- Making data-driven points about how controversies unfold
- Communicating complex findings to diverse audiences

The non-neutrality of visualization is particularly important in controversy mapping, where the way you present actors and their relationships can itself become a matter of debate.
