# 45

*Energinet, stated(†) on LinkedIn (Social media), the 2024-02-06:*

Energinet highlights the unique archaeological insights provided by the Energy Island Bornholm project. Since May 2023, Bornholms Museum has conducted preliminary archaeological investigations at Aakirkeby, where the onshore facility for Energy Island Bornholm will be built. These investigations have already uncovered a wealth of artifacts, including flint tools from the Stone Age, a miniature cannon, and a spindle whorl from the Viking Age.

After a year and 350 exploratory trenches, the first of two investigation phases has been completed. Out of a 120-hectare area surveyed, more than 35 hectares have been identified for excavation, marking the largest archaeological dig ever undertaken on Bornholm. The museum is now planning the timeline and methods for this significant endeavor, which offers an unparalleled opportunity to uncover and preserve knowledge of Bornholm’s history.

The investigations have already mapped cultural landscapes dating back 6,000 years, revealing traces of the Neolithic period, when agriculture arrived via the first farmers crossing the Baltic Sea, as well as artifacts from the Bronze Age, Iron Age, and Viking Age. Finds include flint tools, pottery shards, coins, jewelry, weapons, over 100 houses, settlements, and several graves.

The methods for setting tariffs must adhere to the principle of cost causality, meaning the tariffs must reflect the costs generated by each category of network users. In this context, Energinet has informed that the general tariff method for producer payments specifically excludes electricity production connected to energy islands. This is because the standardized connection fee for electricity production currently in place cannot accurately capture the payment structure that should apply to connections on energy islands. # 1002

*Aliaksei Patonia , Veronika Lenivova , Rahmat Poudineh , Christoph Nolden, The Oxford Institute for Energy Studies (researchers), stated(†) on The Oxford Institute for Energy Studies (Company website), the 2025-01-13:*

As the world races to decarbonize its energy systems, the choice between transmitting green energy as electrons through high-voltage direct current (HVDC) lines or as molecules via hydrogen pipelines emerges as a critical decision. We consider this pivotal choice and compare the techno-economic characteristics of these two transmission technologies.

Hydrogen pipelines offer the advantage of transporting larger energy volumes, but existing projects are dwarfed by the vast networks of HVDC transmission lines. We see potential in expanding hydrogen pipeline networks, capitalizing on hydrogen’s physical similarities to natural gas and the potential for cost savings. However, hydrogen’s unique characteristics, such as its small molecular size and compression requirements, present construction challenges. On the other hand, HVDC lines, while less voluminous, excel in efficiently transmitting green electrons over long distances. We already have an extensive global network of HVDC lines, and their efficiency makes them suitable for various applications. Yet, intermittent renewable energy sources pose challenges for both hydrogen and electricity systems, necessitating solutions like storage and blending.

Considering these technologies as standalone competitors belies their complementary nature. In the emerging energy landscape, we believe both will be integral components of a complex system. Decisions on which technology to prioritize depend on factors such as existing infrastructure, adaptability, risk assessment, and social acceptance. Furthermore, while both HVDC lines and hydrogen pipelines are expected to proliferate, other factors such as market maturity of the relevant energy vector, government policies, and regulatory frameworks around grid development and utilization will also play a crucial role. Energy transition is a multifaceted challenge, and we see accommodating both green molecules and electrons in our energy infrastructure as key to a sustainable future.

*Dominic Machaelis, RIBA (Researcher), stated(†) on Dominic Machaelis (Newspaper), the 2002-06-01:*

Wind energy at sea is at its strongest, unaffected by land masses. The Energy Island has six large aerogenerators, three at a lower height (OMIT) to intercept the sea-hugging wind flow, while three are at greater heights to capture the upper wind flows. The hydraulic masts are able to adjust the heights of the aerogenerators to best harvest the wind potential from different directions.

Apart from its electricity contribution, the Energy Island is designed also to produce hydrogen. Because of the fluctuation of renewable energy resources, an energy storage system is required. The Energy Island relies on a hydrolysis plant to produce stored hydrogen and oxygen, part of which can be used by fuel cell technology as necessary to balance the power output for both external and internal uses. Part of the fuel can, in some cases, be tankered or pipelined to other destinations, when the electricity produced is not totally used by cable links to nearby destinations.

The Energy Island will also produce a base for Controlled Environment Agriculture (CEA) and Mariculture. In tropical latitudes, greenhouses need to be protected from the sun, a role performed by the heliostats, and well ventilated or evaporatively cooled. The 22-hectare platform can therefore be used for CEA, using part of the distilled water from the open OTEC cycle for hydroponics. If the floor is trellised, light can reach the seawater below, where pens would contain various forms of marine life.

Harbours and Housing: On the leeward side of the island or islands, the Energy Island needs to provide housing for staff, social facilities, workshops and harbours.

The Energy Island uses many diverse renewable energy sources, bringing their converted powers together in the most efficient way to provide electricity for the grid, and, by hydrolysis, hydrogen fuel for transport to nearby or distant population centers, where it can be used efficiently by fuel cell technology. The Energy Island can become a major supplier of electricity and hydrogen fuel for a pollution free society, and liberate the world from the perils of nuclear power generation, and from its political, economic and polluting dependence on dwindling fossil fuels. The Energy Island therefore constitutes a new departure, demonstrated to be all the more necessary by the visible signs of pollution and global warming, and the frightening fact that world energy use is expanding exponentially, having doubled in the last thirty years. Action is needed to bring about change and turn the tide.

• The technical design of the project and the economic consequences of connecting the cable to Bornholm

• Technical consequences for the electricity system in terms of stability and robustness
• Possibility of co-location, e.g., placement of future PtX facilities
• Perspectives for the long-term development of the energy system

*Lars Aagaard, Danish parliament, Moderaterne (Current Minister for Climate, Energy and Utilities), stated(†) on Danish ministry of climate, energy and utilities (Governmental report), the 2023-06-19:*

The Climate, Energy, and Utilities Committee has asked me, in a letter dated June 1, 2023, to answer the following question, which was requested by Steffen W. Frølund (LA). The question concerns the consequences of applying the same tariff model, expected to be used for Energiø Bornholm, to all renewable energy (RE) electricity producers connected at the transmission level.

I must first note that according to the market directive, it is the collective electricity supply companies that develop tariff methods, and the Energy Regulatory Authority (Forsyningstilsynet) that approves them. This means that the Minister or the Parliament has very limited ability to dictate tariff designs.

The tariff pricing methods must, among other things, adhere to the principle of cost-reflectiveness, meaning they must reflect the costs incurred by the various categories of network users. In connection with this, Energinet has informed me that the general tariff model for producer payments specifically excludes electricity production connected to energy islands. This is because the standardized connection fee now applied for electricity production cannot, in a cost-reflective manner, accommodate the payment required for connection to energy islands. The transmission network at and near energy islands is of a special nature, as it includes both trade connections to foreign countries and specialized installations for connecting offshore wind, which also involves significantly higher investment costs compared to, for example, the connection of a typical offshore wind farm or larger solar park.

As a result, Energinet is currently developing a tariff financing model for Energinet’s costs related to energy islands. This model was sent for public consultation on May 3, 2023, with a response deadline of June 6, 2023. The draft tariff model proposes that the offshore wind connected to an energy island will be tariffed according to the costs it incurs. This draft includes, among other things, that electricity producers and consumers will share the financing of parts of Energinet’s costs using a distribution key specially developed for financing Energinet’s costs related to energy islands in order to allocate these costs in a cost-reflective manner.

Such a distribution key cannot be applied to tariffing other RE electricity producers, as they do not contribute to the financing of trade connections as part of their connection to the grid. This is because other RE electricity producers are not directly dependent on specific trade connections in their grid connection and the sale of their production, like the offshore wind connected at Energiø Bornholm.
